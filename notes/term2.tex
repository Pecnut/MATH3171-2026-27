\chapter{PDE stability: populations, boundaries and spatial modes}
\label{merged:pde}
This term brings together two strands of mathematics you have already studied: linear stability of population models in Mathematical Biology, and eigenfunction expansions for PDEs in \emph{Mathematical Methods II (MATH2811)}. Our new biological question is how an initially almost uniform population or chemical mixture can develop an organised spatial pattern.

Before we discuss particular patterns, we need to connect those two pieces of mathematics carefully. The basic idea is simple. We first linearise the biological model about a spatially homogeneous equilibrium, exactly as we linearised ODE models last term. We then decompose the resulting spatial perturbation into the eigenfunctions used in Mathematical Methods. Because those spatial functions are linearly independent, the PDE separates into one small system of ODEs for each spatial mode. The rest of the term is largely about understanding when one of those modal systems grows.

\section{What problem are we linearising?}\label{merged:generalrd}
A general reaction--diffusion model for $m$ interacting populations or chemical species can be written
\begin{equation}\label{merged:nonlinearRD}
\pd{\mathbf u}{t}=\mathcal D\nabla^2\mathbf u+\mathbf F(\mathbf u),
\qquad
\mathbf u(\v{x},t)=
\begin{pmatrix}u_1(\v{x},t)\\ \vdots\\ u_m(\v{x},t)\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The vector $\mathbf F$ describes the local biological or chemical interactions: growth, competition, activation, inhibition, reaction, and so on. The diffusion matrix $\mathcal D$ describes spatial movement. In the simplest models it is diagonal,
\[
\mathcal D=\operatorname{diag}(D_1,\ldots,D_m),
\]
so that species $j$ diffuses with coefficient $D_j$. The scalar case is included by taking $m=1$. At the end of this chapter, we will use that case to show why interacting species are needed for the diffusion-driven instability studied in Chapter~\ref{turinganalysis}.

Suppose $\mathbf u_*$ is a spatially homogeneous equilibrium. Since it is constant in space, $\nabla^2\mathbf u_*=0$, and therefore its reaction terms must satisfy
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\mathbf u_*)=\mathbf 0.
\end{equation}
For fixed-value boundary conditions, the equilibrium must of course also be compatible with the imposed boundary values.

We now ask the usual stability question: if the system is disturbed slightly from $\mathbf u_*$, does the disturbance decay or grow? Write
\begin{equation}
\mathbf u(\v{x},t)=\mathbf u_*+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\eta}(\v{x},t),
\qquad 0<\varepsilon\ll1.
\end{equation}
Taylor expansion of the reaction terms gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbf F(\mathbf u_*+\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\eta})
=\mathbf F(\mathbf u_*)+\varepsilon J\boldsymbol{\eta}+O(\varepsilon^2),
\qquad
J=\left.\frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\mathbf u}\right|_{\mathbf u_*}.
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf F(\mathbf u_*)=0$, substitution into \cref{merged:nonlinearRD}, division by $\varepsilon$, and retention of the leading terms gives the \emph{linearised reaction--diffusion equation}
\begin{equation}\label{merged:linearRD}
\pd{\boldsymbol{\eta}}{t}=\mathcal D\nabla^2\boldsymbol{\eta}+J\boldsymbol{\eta}.
\end{equation}
Thus $J$ is exactly the reaction Jacobian familiar from the ODE stability calculations of last term. The only new ingredient is that the perturbation also varies in space and diffuses.

The question is therefore no longer just ``which eigenvalues does $J$ have?'' A spatial disturbance can have many different shapes. We need a way to split an arbitrary perturbation into simple spatial pieces and ask how each one evolves. This is where the eigenfunction methods from Mathematical Methods enter.

\section{From Mathematical Methods to mathematical biology}\label{merged:methodsbridge}
\begin{info}
\textbf{A quick route back into Mathematical Methods II.}
It may have been some time since you used these ideas. Chapter~2, \S2.2 (separation of variables) introduces the spatial eigenfunctions, while \S2.3 shows that the same spatial mode can decay or grow depending on its time equation. Chapter~4, \S4.2 develops the same idea using eigenfunction expansions. For orthogonality, projection and linear independence, see Chapter~1, \S\S1.2.2--1.2.3.
\end{info}

Start with the scalar heat equation
\begin{equation}\label{merged:heatrecap}
\pd{u}{t}=D\nabla^2u
\end{equation}
on a fixed bounded domain with homogeneous boundary conditions. The corresponding spatial eigenfunctions satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{merged:spatialeigenrecap}
-\nabla^2w_n=\rho_n w_n.
\end{equation}
For the standard Dirichlet and Neumann problems these eigenfunctions are orthogonal. Nonzero orthogonal functions are automatically linearly independent, and under the conditions used in Mathematical Methods they form a complete basis for the functions we want to represent. We therefore write
\begin{equation}\label{merged:scalarrecapexpansion}
u(\v{x},t)=\sum_n c_n(t)w_n(\v{x}).
\end{equation}

It is worth recalling explicitly why this turns a PDE into separate ODEs. Differentiating the expansion in time and using \cref{merged:spatialeigenrecap} gives
\[
\pd{u}{t}=\sum_n\frac{\mathrm dc_n}{\mathrm dt}w_n,
\qquad
D\nabla^2u=-\sum_nD\rho_nc_nw_n.
\]
Hence the heat equation becomes
\begin{equation}\label{merged:independencerecap}
\sum_n\left(\frac{\mathrm dc_n}{\mathrm dt}+D\rho_nc_n\right)w_n=0.
\end{equation}
Because the $w_n$ are \emph{linearly independent}, the only way this identity can hold for every point in space is for every coefficient to vanish separately:
\begin{equation}\label{merged:scalarode}
\frac{\mathrm dc_n}{\mathrm dt}=-D\rho_nc_n
\qquad\hbox{for every }n.
\end{equation}
This is the key step to remember. Orthogonality gives us a particularly convenient way to calculate or project out coefficients; the separation into independent modal equations ultimately follows from linear independence. Each complicated spatial solution is built by adding together simple modes, each with its own time evolution.

Now return to the linearised biological system \cref{merged:linearRD}. When all components satisfy the same type of homogeneous spatial boundary condition, we expand every component in the same spatial eigenfunctions:
\begin{equation}\label{merged:vectorexpansionrecap}
\boldsymbol{\eta}(\v{x},t)=\sum_n\boldsymbol{a}_n(t)w_n(\v{x}),
\qquad
\boldsymbol{a}_n(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}a_{1n}(t)\\ \vdots\\ a_{mn}(t)\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Substitution into \cref{merged:linearRD} gives
\[
\sum_n
\left[
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol{a}_n}{\mathrm dt}
-\left(J-\rho_n\mathcal D\right)\boldsymbol{a}_n
\right]w_n=\mathbf 0.
\]
Again the $w_n$ are linearly independent, so the coefficient of each spatial mode must vanish:
\begin{equation}\label{merged:transfer}
\frac{\mathrm d\boldsymbol{a}_n}{\mathrm dt}
=\left(J-\rho_n\mathcal D\right)\boldsymbol{a}_n.
\end{equation}

This is the central transfer from Mathematical Methods. For the scalar heat equation, each spatial mode gave one scalar ODE. For $m$ interacting species, each spatial mode gives an $m$-dimensional coupled ODE system. Different spatial modes remain separate in the \emph{linearised} problem, but the species amplitudes within one mode are coupled through the reaction Jacobian $J$.

The boundary conditions determine which spatial functions $w_n$ and spatial eigenvalues $\rho_n$ are allowed. The matrix $J-\rho_n\mathcal D$ then determines whether that particular spatial pattern grows or decays. For an initial-value problem, orthogonality lets us project the initial disturbance onto the different $w_n$ to find the starting amplitudes. For a stability calculation, we usually do not need those amplitudes: it is enough to ask whether \emph{any} admissible mode has a temporal growth rate with positive real part.

\begin{info}
\textbf{A notation translation.} Mathematical Methods uses $\lambda_n$ for spatial eigenvalues and, in its discussion of growing modes, $\sigma$ for a temporal growth rate. In these Mathematical Biology notes the corresponding quantities are $\rho_n$ and $\lambda$:
\[
\lambda_n^{\text{Methods}}=\rho_n^{\text{Biology}},\qquad
\sigma^{\text{Methods}}=\lambda^{\text{Biology}}.
\]
The spatial problem supplies $\rho_n$; for two species the mode matrix supplies two temporal rates $\lambda_{n,+}$ and $\lambda_{n,-}$. A nonnegative spatial eigenvalue does not imply a growing perturbation.
\end{info}

One final qualification will matter later. The clean separation into independent spatial modes is a property of the \emph{linearised} equations. Nonlinear terms contain products of perturbations and therefore generally mix different spatial modes together. Linear mode analysis tells us which patterns initially grow from a small disturbance; nonlinear dynamics determine what happens once those disturbances are no longer small.

We now apply this framework to a spatial competitive Lotka--Volterra model. The calculation will be familiar, but the biological role of the boundary conditions is new: competition, diffusion and habitat size can together determine whether a population survives.

\section{A spatial population model}
Consider the nondimensional system
\begin{align}\label{merged:population}
\pd{u}{t}&=\pdd{u}{x}+u(1-u-\gamma_1v),\\
\pd{v}{t}&=\kappa\pdd{v}{x}+\beta v(1-v-\gamma_2u),
\end{align}
on $0<x<L$, with $\kappa,\beta,\gamma_1,\gamma_2>0$. The parameters $\kappa$ and $\beta$ compare diffusion and growth rates, respectively, while the $\gamma_i$ measure competition. A spatially homogeneous equilibrium $(u_0,v_0)$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
u_0(1-u_0-\gamma_1v_0)=0,\qquad
v_0(1-v_0-\gamma_2u_0)=0.
\end{equation}
These are the same equilibrium equations as for the corresponding ODE model. In a PDE, however, the equilibrium must also satisfy the boundary conditions.

For fixed-value boundaries compatible with this equilibrium, we impose
\begin{equation}
u(0,t)=u(L,t)=u_0,\qquad v(0,t)=v(L,t)=v_0.
\end{equation}
Writing $u=u_0+\varepsilon u_1$ and $v=v_0+\varepsilon v_1$, the perturbations therefore satisfy \emph{zero} boundary values. The boundary values of the equilibrium are not the boundary values of the perturbation. This is the same subtraction of boundary data used in Mathematical Methods \S3.2.2 and \S4.2; here the subtracted function is the constant equilibrium.

\section{Linearisation and the mode matrix}
This is now the concrete two-species version of the general calculation above, with $\mathbf u=(u,v)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\mathcal D=\operatorname{diag}(1,\kappa)$. Keeping terms of order $\varepsilon$ gives
\begin{align}
\pd{u_1}{t}&=\pdd{u_1}{x}+(1-2u_0-\gamma_1v_0)u_1-\gamma_1u_0v_1,\\
\pd{v_1}{t}&=\kappa\pdd{v_1}{x}-\beta\gamma_2v_0u_1+
\beta(1-2v_0-\gamma_2u_0)v_1.
\end{align}
From Mathematical Methods \S2.2.2, zero Dirichlet boundary values select the spatial functions
\begin{equation}\label{merged:dirichletmodes}
w_n(x)=\sin(n\pi x/L),\qquad
\rho_n=(n\pi/L)^2,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,
\end{equation}
which satisfy $-w_n''=\rho_nw_n$. Seek one mode in the form
\begin{equation}\label{ansatz}
\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\v_1\end{pmatrix}
=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}w_n(x)\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The two components use the same spatial eigenfunction because the reaction terms couple them. Substitution yields
\begin{equation}\label{merged:modematrix}
\lambda\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
1-2u_0-\gamma_1v_0-\rho_n&-\gamma_1u_0\\
-\beta\gamma_2v_0&\beta(1-2v_0-\gamma_2u_0)-\kappa\rho_n
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Thus the growth rates are the eigenvalues of
$M_n=J-\rho_n\operatorname{diag}(1,\kappa)$, where $J$ is the reaction Jacobian. This is the familiar ODE calculation, but repeated for every spatial mode allowed by the boundaries.

\begin{info}
Keep three quantities distinct: $n$ labels a mode; $\rho_n$ is its spatial eigenvalue; and $\lambda_{n,+},\lambda_{n,-}$ are its two temporal growth rates. In one dimension $n\pi/L$ is the wavenumber and $\rho_n$ is its square.
\end{info}

An arbitrary sufficiently regular perturbation can be expanded as
\begin{equation}\label{merged:expansion}
\begin{pmatrix}u_1(x,t)\\v_1(x,t)\end{pmatrix}
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{a}_n(t)w_n(x),
\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{a}_n}{\mathrm{d}t}=M_n\boldsymbol{a}_n.
\end{equation}
Linear independence separates the spatial modes; orthogonality provides the convenient projection onto them. Within each mode, the two populations remain coupled. When $M_n$ is diagonalisable its solution contains both eigenvalue branches. Stability requires both growth rates to have negative real part for every admissible mode; one positive growth rate is sufficient for linear instability. Zero growth rates require further analysis.

\section{Extinction and the size of a habitat}\label{harsh_environment}
At $(u_0,v_0)=(0,0)$ the mode matrix is diagonal, so
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{n,1}=1-\rho_n,\qquad
\lambda_{n,2}=\beta-\kappa\rho_n.
\end{equation}
Both must be negative. Since $\rho_n$ increases with $n$, the most restrictive mode here is $n=1$. Hence extinction is linearly asymptotically stable if
\begin{equation}\label{merged:extinction}
L<\pi\quad\hbox{and}\quad L<\pi\sqrt{\kappa/\beta},
\quad\hbox{or equivalently}\quad
L<\pi\min\{1,\sqrt{\kappa/\beta}\}.
\end{equation}
The word \emph{and} matters: decay of one population does not compensate for growth of the other. Without spatial losses, both populations grow from a small positive density. With zero population at the boundaries, diffusion transports individuals towards a hostile exterior. On a sufficiently small habitat, these losses dominate reproduction.

Figure~\ref{extinctionzero} illustrates decay and growth on either side of a domain-size threshold. Here, and later, numerical solutions of the nonlinear model provide a check on what the linear calculation predicts near equilibrium.

\subsection{What changes with no-flux boundaries?}
Recall the Neumann case in Mathematical Methods \S2.2.2. If $u_x=v_x=0$ at $x=0,L$, the eigenfunctions are
\begin{equation}\label{merged:neumannmodes}
w_n(x)=\cos(n\pi x/L),\qquad \rho_n=(n\pi/L)^2,
\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
\end{equation}
The matrix calculation is unchanged, but the allowed modes are different. In particular, $w_0=1$ and $\rho_0=0$ describe a uniform perturbation. At extinction this mode has growth rates $1$ and $\beta$, both positive. Extinction is therefore unstable for every $L>0$ in the closed habitat. This conclusion follows directly from the zero mode, without manipulating a domain-size inequality derived for $n\geq1$.

\subsection{Invasion of a resident population}
At $(u_0,v_0)=(0,1)$ the matrix is triangular and its growth rates are
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{n,1}=1-\gamma_1-\rho_n,\qquad
\lambda_{n,2}=-\beta-\kappa\rho_n.
\end{equation}
The second is always negative. With fixed boundary values $u=0$, $v=1$, stability therefore requires
$1-\gamma_1-(\pi/L)^2<0$.
For $\gamma_1\geq1$ this holds for every finite $L$; for $\gamma_1<1$ it requires $L<\pi/\sqrt{1-\gamma_1}$. With no-flux boundaries the zero mode is allowed, and strict linear stability requires $\gamma_1>1$. Biologically, invasion depends on competition with the resident population and, for the fixed-value problem, losses through the boundaries.

\section{A scalar comparison and the spatial eigenvalue viewpoint}
For reference, the scalar logistic model
\begin{equation}\label{rd_eqn_logistic}
\pd{u}{t}=D\pdd{u}{x}+ru(1-u/K)
\end{equation}
has perturbation growth rates $\lambda_n=r-D\rho_n$ at extinction. With zero Dirichlet boundary values,
\begin{equation}\label{harsh_environment_growth}
\lambda_1<0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L<\pi\sqrt{D/r}.
\end{equation}
With no-flux boundaries, $\lambda_0=r>0$. This is the one-population version of the previous calculation. Above the Dirichlet threshold, nonzero nonnegative populations can approach a positive spatially varying steady state; its profile is constrained by the zero boundary values, as illustrated below.

On a general bounded domain we write the spatial eigenvalue problem as
\begin{equation}\label{merged:laplacian}
-\nabla^2w_k=\rho_kw_k,
\end{equation}
with the chosen boundary conditions. For homogeneous Dirichlet or Neumann conditions the spatial eigenvalues are nonnegative, can be listed with multiplicity, and tend to infinity. On a connected domain, Neumann conditions include a constant eigenfunction with $\rho_0=0$; Dirichlet conditions do not. Different eigenfunctions may share the same eigenvalue.

The product construction from Mathematical Methods \S1.2.5 and \S4.5.3 gives the higher-dimensional modes directly. In two dimensions, on $[0,L_1]\times[0,L_2]$, the Neumann eigenfunctions are
\begin{equation}
w_{n,m}=\cos(n\pi x_1/L_1)\cos(m\pi x_2/L_2),\quad
\rho_{n,m}=(n\pi/L_1)^2+(m\pi/L_2)^2,
\end{equation}
with $n,m\geq0$. For homogeneous Dirichlet conditions, replace cosines by sines and require $n,m\geq1$.

For a reaction--diffusion system with constant diffusion matrix $\mathcal D$, a homogeneous equilibrium and common homogeneous spatial boundary conditions, each spatial mode is governed by $J-\rho_k\mathcal D$. The kinetics determine the growth-rate relation; the geometry and boundaries determine where it is sampled.

\section{From spatial modes to biological patterns}
The purpose of the calculation is to explain an observed structure. A spatial eigenfunction describes a concentration variation; a visible spot, stripe or hair requires an additional biological response to that variation.

If $u=u_0+\varepsilon u_1$, a condition $u>u_{\mathrm{th}}$ becomes $u_1>(u_{\mathrm{th}}-u_0)/\varepsilon$. Negative values of $u_1$ represent concentrations below the equilibrium value, not negative physical concentrations. A restricted band of positive growth rates favours some spatial scales over others, and faster-growing modes may dominate the early pattern if they are present initially. Linear growth eventually invalidates the small-perturbation approximation. Nonlinear dynamics determine whether growth saturates, which patterns persist, and their final amplitude.

Geometry changes the eigenfunctions: on circular or annular domains, radial Bessel functions combine with angular sines and cosines. The resulting patterns can form rings or repeated features around a circumference. We will return to this connection when modelling hair whorls in \emph{Acetabularia}. These pictures show what spatial modes can look like. Before asking which modes grow, we should establish why interactions between species matter for their growth.


\section{Why do we need interacting species?}\label{merged:scalarbridge}
We have used vector notation to treat several species together. Before developing Turing's mechanism, let us ask whether those interactions are really necessary. Could one species, with local growth or reactions and ordinary Fickian diffusion, develop a pattern by destabilising a stable uniform state?

\subsection*{One species: diffusion only reduces the growth rates}
Consider a single concentration or population density satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{merged:scalarRD}
\pd{u}{t}=D\pdd{u}{x}+f(u),\qquad D>0,\qquad 0<x<L,
\end{equation}
with no-flux boundaries $u_x(0,t)=u_x(L,t)=0$. Here the Fickian flux is $-D u_x$: individuals or material move down concentration gradients. A homogeneous equilibrium $u_*$ satisfies $f(u_*)=0$. Without spatial variation the model is simply $\dot u=f(u)$, so this equilibrium is linearly asymptotically stable if $f'(u_*)<0$.

Set $u=u_*+\varepsilon\eta$ and retain terms of order $\varepsilon$. Then
\[
\pd{\eta}{t}=D\pdd{\eta}{x}+f'(u_*)\eta.
\]
Using the no-flux modes from \cref{merged:neumannmodes}, write
\[
\eta(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(t)\cos(n\pi x/L).
\]
Substitution and linear independence give, just as in our Mathematical Methods recap,
\begin{equation}\label{merged:scalarnoturing}
\dot a_n=\lambda_n a_n,\qquad
\lambda_n=f'(u_*)-D\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The constant mode $n=0$ has growth rate $\lambda_0=f'(u_*)$. Every nonuniform mode has an additional negative contribution from diffusion. Consequently,
\[
\lambda_n\leq\lambda_0<0\qquad\hbox{for every }n.
\]
Thus, if uniform disturbances decay, all spatial disturbances decay too. If instead $f'(u_*)>0$, the uniform mode is already unstable and grows faster than any nonuniform mode: diffusion has not created an instability. On a general domain the same argument reads $\lambda_n=f'(u_*)-D\rho_n$, with $\rho_n\geq0$.

\subsection*{Example: logistic growth in a closed habitat}
Return to \cref{rd_eqn_logistic}, with $f(u)=ru(1-u/K)$ and $r,K>0$, but now impose no-flux boundaries. The positive homogeneous equilibrium is $u_*=K$, and
\[
f'(K)=-r,\qquad
\lambda_n=-r-D\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2<0.
\]
For example, a small initial variation $u(x,0)=K+\varepsilon\cos(\pi x/L)$ evolves, to linear order, as
\[
u(x,t)=K+\varepsilon\mathrm e^{-[r+D(\pi/L)^2]t}\cos(\pi x/L).
\]
Local logistic growth restores the carrying capacity, while diffusion smooths out the spatial variation. Increasing $D$ makes this variation disappear faster; it cannot make it grow.

\begin{info}
\textbf{What have we ruled out?}
A single scalar reaction--diffusion equation with constant positive Fickian diffusivity cannot undergo a \emph{Turing instability}: a homogeneous equilibrium that is linearly asymptotically stable without diffusion cannot be destabilised by adding diffusion. This is a statement about growth of small disturbances of that equilibrium, not a claim that scalar PDEs can never have spatial structure.
\end{info}

For example, the positive logistic profiles in \cref{dirichlet_rd_fig} arise with hostile, zero-density boundaries. Those boundaries exclude the homogeneous state $u=K$, and extinction is already unstable in the well-mixed kinetics. These profiles therefore do not contradict the calculation above. Nonlinear scalar systems can also support fronts between different states. With bistable reactions, suitable non-convex domains can even support stable nonuniform equilibria, as illustrated in \cref{Dumbbell}; these likewise need not arise by destabilising a stable homogeneous equilibrium.

\subsection*{What changes when species interact?}
For two species, the scalar number $f'(u_*)$ is replaced by a reaction Jacobian $J$, and each spatial mode is governed by
\[
M_n=J-\rho_n\begin{pmatrix}D_1&0\\0&D_2\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Its growth rates are the eigenvalues of this \emph{coupled matrix}. We cannot in general obtain them by subtracting $D_1\rho_n$ and $D_2\rho_n$ from the two eigenvalues of $J$ separately. If $D_1=D_2=D$, however, $M_n=J-D\rho_n I$: both eigenvalues shift left by the same amount, so a stable $J$ remains stable. Unequal diffusion can change the balance of activation and inhibition between interacting species, allowing a spatial mode to grow even though a uniform disturbance decays.

This explains why we introduced vectors: multiple interacting species provide a mechanism unavailable to one scalar density. Interactions and unequal diffusion do not guarantee a pattern; they make a Turing instability possible. Chapter~\ref{turinganalysis} determines precisely when the reaction kinetics and diffusion rates allow it.

\section{How can interactions make diffusion destabilising?}
The scalar calculation in \cref{merged:scalarbridge} gives us a clear target: stable uniform disturbances, but at least one growing nonuniform mode. A single species cannot achieve this through ordinary Fickian diffusion, and neither can a system in which all species diffuse at the same rate. We now investigate two interacting species with different diffusion rates.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{images2/FoodColouring} \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images2/Dye}
    \caption{An example of diffusion smearing out spatial structures of dye in water. When dye is dropped into water, it often forms interesting spatial structures of moving tendrils and swirls. Over time, however, everything settles into a homogeneous mixture, where the dye has approximately the same concentration everywhere. This is due to diffusion mixing everything up evenly.}
    \label{FoodColouring}
\end{figure}

Diffusion still smooths the concentration of each species, as the dye illustration suggests. Turing's insight was that this smoothing can alter the balance between coupled reactions: species that jointly restore equilibrium in a well-mixed system need not do so for every spatial disturbance. We will turn this possibility into explicit inequalities on the reaction Jacobian and the diffusion rates. The resulting linear analysis identifies which spatial modes initially grow; the nonlinear equations determine their subsequent amplitude and whether a persistent pattern forms.

\section{Linear stability analysis of reaction--diffusion systems}
The modal framework is already in place: see \cref{merged:methodsbridge} and Mathematical Methods \S\S2.3, 4.2. We now retain the detailed matrix calculation because the new issue is whether stable reaction kinetics can become unstable when diffusion is added.
We now consider the following general reaction--diffusion system,\footnote{The, generally nonlinear, functions $F$ and $G$ are quite arbitrary, though one needs some technical assumptions on their growth rates to ensure that solutions exist for all time etc. We will not dwell on these technicalities, but always assume these functions are sufficiently `nice' to guarantee what we need. Generally systems like the functions we've seen in the ODE examples from last term work well enough.} 
\begin{align}
\pd{u}{t} &= D_1\nabla^2u + F(u,v),\\
\pd{v}{t} &= D_2\nabla^2v + G(u,v).
\end{align}
and make the scalings\footnote{In Murray's book, and in previous versions of these notes, the timescale was nondimensionalised as $\hat{t} = t/\gamma$ and the $\v{x}$ scale was also scaled by this timescale. For notational simplicity, we do not choose a timescale, so if you look at past exams or additional questions you can remember that here we've taken $\gamma=1$.} $\hat{\v{x}} =\v{x}/\sqrt{D_1}$, and $D = D_2/D_1$, so that upon substituting and dropping the hat notation we have.
\begin{align}\label{RD_System}
\pd{u}{t} &= \nabla^2u +  F(u,v),\\
\pd{v}{t} &= D\nabla^2v +  G(u,v).
\end{align}
The parameter $D>0$, the nondimensionalised ratio of diffusion rates of $u$ and $v$, e.g.\ if $D<1$ then $u$ diffuses faster than $v$ and vice-versa.  The term `reaction' is not always literal as $F$ and $G$ can be the interaction of the two densities $u$ and $v$, which could be populations (predator prey, competition, etc), biochemical reactions, or electrochemical effects, such as in neural signal propagation. It can also represent independent growth/decay or self competition terms. For simplicity in what follows, we will refer to $F$ and $G$ as reactions or sometimes the `reaction kinetics'. This is the language Turing used, as he was really thinking of chemicals as a model for how genetic information determined spatial structure in an embryo.

Unless otherwise stated, we will assume no-flux  boundary conditions; if our domain is $\Omega$ with $\hat{\v{n}}$ the normal to the boundary $\partial \Omega$, then $\vdel u\cdot \hat{\v{n}}=0$ and $\vdel v\cdot \hat{\v{n}}=0$. This implies nothing can enter or leave the system and any pattern formation is due to internal changes in the system. For the homogeneous equilibrium considered here, $u=u_0$ and $v=v_0$ throughout the domain. We will firstly consider the cuboidal domain $\Omega = [0,L_1]\times[0,L_2]\times\dots [0,L_n]$.

The Turing analysis is essentially a linear stability analysis around a spatially homogeneous equilibrium, though we will want to look specifically for conditions when a \emph{spatial} instability occurs which would not occur in the absence of diffusion. Using the notation from last chapter, this means we want $\Re(\lambda_0)<0$ so that the equilibrium is stable in the absence of diffusion, but we are aiming for $\Re(\lambda_k)>0$ for some $k>0$ so that some inhomogeneous mode destabilises our spatially homogeneous equilibrium.


\subsection*{Find the spatially homogeneous equilibria}
We are interested in the stability of \emph{homogeneous} equilibria. As in the population model in the previous chapter, this means we look for constant solutions which satisfy the system of PDEs given by \cref{RD_System}. As before, \emph{homogenous} implies that $u_0$ and $v_0$ have no variation in $x$, i.e.\ $\pdn{n}{u_0}{x_i}=0=\pdn{n}{v_0}{x_i}$ for all $n$ and in all spatial directions $i$. Such equilibria occur when 
\begin{align}
F(u_0,v_0) = 0,\quad G(u_0,v_0)= 0.
\end{align}
\subsection*{Linearise the system}
The linearisation of the derivative terms should be obvious by now, the reaction functions are expanded using a Taylor series
\begin{align}
F(u,v) = \varepsilon \left( u_1\pd{F}{u}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0}   + v_1\pd{F}{v}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0} \right) + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^2),\\
G(u,v) = \varepsilon\left(u_1\pd{G}{u}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0} + v_1\pd{G}{v}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0} \right) + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^2),
\end{align}
where the $\mathcal{O}(1)$ contribution has vanished because we expand about a homogeneous equilibrium. For notational brevity in what follows we denote partial derivatives evaluated at equilibrium with a subscript, e.g.\
\begin{equation}
\pd{F}{u}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0} = F_u,\quad \pd{G}{v}\bigg\vert_{u=u_0,v=v_0} = G_v.
\end{equation}
so that (again remembering that $F(u_0,v_0)=G(u_0,v_0)=0)$
\begin{align}
F(u,v) = \varepsilon \left(F_u  u_1  + F_v v_1 \right) + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^2),\\
G(u,v) = \varepsilon\left(G_u  u_1  + G_v v_1  \right) + \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^2).
\end{align}

So perturbations of the form $u = u_0 +\varepsilon u_1(x,t)$, $v = v_0 + \varepsilon v_1(x,t)$ satisfy:
\begin{align}
\label{RD_System_Lin}
\pd{u_1}{t} &= \nabla^2 u_1 + F_u u_1 + F_v v_1, \\
\pd{v_1}{t} &= D\nabla^2 v_1 + G_u u_1 + G_v v_1.
\end{align}
Or, equivalently, using the vector $\v{u}_1 = (u_1,v_1)^T$,
\begin{equation}\label{linear_turing}
    \pd{\v{u}_1}{t} = \m{D}\nabla^2\v{u}_1 + \m{J}\v{u}_1, \quad \m{D} = \mat{1}{0}{0}{D}, \quad \m{J} = \mat{F_u}{F_v}{G_u}{G_v}.
\end{equation}
We can solve this system exactly as we did the scalar case -- by exploiting eigenfunctions of the Laplacian and using the same exponential ansatz in time as before.

\subsection*{Solve the linearised system}

On the cuboidal domain, use a Neumann eigenfunction $w_{\v{k}}(\v{x})$, a product of cosines with $k_i=n_i\pi/L_i$ and $n_i\geq0$. A single temporal eigenmode has the form $\v{u}_1=\e^{\lambda_{\v{k}}t}w_{\v{k}}\boldsymbol{a}$. As $-\nabla^2w_{\v{k}}=|\v{k}|^2w_{\v{k}}$, the amplitude vector obeys the following matrix eigenvalue problem (with its components denoted by $u_1,v_1$ in this display):
\begin{equation}
\label{turinglin}
\lambda_\v{k}\mvec{u_1}{v_1} =\underbrace{\mat{-\ks+   F_u}{ F_v}{ G_u}{-D\ks + G_v}}_{\m{M}}\mvec{u_1}{v_1}
\end{equation}
or $\lambda_\v{k} \v{u}_1 = \m{M}\v{u}_1 = (-\ks\m{D} + \m{J})\v{u}_1$.  For notational brevity in what follows we write $\rho_k = |\v{k}|^2 = \v{k}\cdot\v{k} = k_1^2 + k_2^2 + \dots k_n^2$. We call $\ks$ the \emph{spatial eigenvalue} (the squared wavenumber on a rectangular domain). The numerical value of $\ks$ determines an average frequency of oscillations of perturbations. Functions with small $\ks$ have long wavelength oscillations, whereas those with very large $\ks$ will have very fast, short wavelength oscillations.

 Solving the eigenequation $\det(\m{M}-\lambda_\v{k} \m{I}) = 0$ we have a quadratic equation for $\lambda_\v{k}$ given by:
 \begin{equation}
     \lambda^2_\v{k} -\tr(\m{M})\lambda + \det(\m{M})=0.
 \end{equation}
 This has the two solutions,
\begin{equation}
\label{turinglambda}
\lambda_\v{k}  = \frac{1}{2}\left[\tr(\m{M}) \pm \sqrt{\tr(\m{M})^2 -4\det(\m{M})}\right]
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{align}
\nonumber\tr(\m{M}) &= (F_u +G_v)-\ks(D+1),\\
\label{fulldet}\det(\m{M}) &= D\ks^2  -  \ks(G_v+ D F_u)+ (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v).
\end{align}
So given a particular wavenumber $\v{k}$, the equilibrium is stable to a perturbation of that form if $\tr(\m{M})<0$ and $\det(\m{M})>0$. In contrast, we have instability if either $\tr(\m{M})>0$ or $\det{\m{M}}< 0$. Of course, we have also expanded around a single mode, and there will be countably infinite numbers of modes, given by the wave vector $\v{k}$. 

\subsection{Boundary conditions and the homogeneous mode}
The opening chapter already established the role of boundary conditions. On a rectangular domain, no-flux conditions give products of cosines with
\begin{equation}\label{kcon}
\boldsymbol{k}=(n_1\pi/L_1,\ldots,n_d\pi/L_d),\qquad n_i=0,1,2,\ldots,
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}\label{mode_numbers}
\rho_k=|\boldsymbol{k}|^2=\pi^2\sum_{i=1}^d n_i^2/L_i^2.
\end{equation}
The constant mode has $\rho_0=0$ and is governed by the reaction Jacobian $J$. For a Turing instability it must decay, so we require
\begin{equation}\label{homogcons}
F_u+G_v<0,\qquad F_uG_v-F_vG_u>0.
\end{equation}
These are precisely the stability conditions for
\begin{align}\label{RD_System_ODE}
\fd{u}{t}&=F(u,v),\\
\fd{v}{t}&=G(u,v).
\end{align}
For fixed boundary values compatible with the equilibrium, the perturbations instead have zero Dirichlet values. Products of sines are required, all $n_i$ are positive, and the constant mode is absent. Our definition of a Turing instability nevertheless compares the spatial system with the well-mixed reaction kinetics; boundary-induced instabilities should be distinguished from that mechanism.

\subsection{Growing inhomogeneous modes}
Next we seek for inhomogeneous unstable modes $\v{k} = \left(n_1\pi/L_1,n_2\pi/L_2,\dots n_n\pi/L_n\right)$ which are unstable, so they don't vanish and instead grow (at least one $\lambda_\v{k}$ has positive real part). Since $(F_u+G_v)<0$ the trace  $(F_u +G_v)-\rho_k(D+1)$  is less than zero. Thus we can only have positive $\lambda_\v{k}$ if $\det(\m{M})<0$ so that one of the roots must be positive, that is to say 
\begin{equation}\label{h_eq}
 h(\rho_k) = D\rho_k^2  -  \rho_k(G_v+ D F_u)+ (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) < 0.
\end{equation}
Since $D\rho_k^2>0$  and $(F_uG_v  -G_uF_v)>0$  (so the zeroth order mode vanishes), this can only occur if 
\begin{equation}
G_v+ D F_u>0.
\end{equation}

This condition is necessary but not sufficient to guarantee that $\det(\m{M})<0$ for some $(\rho_k\neq 0)$. To help understand this, look at panel (a) of \cref{disp_rel_fig}, ignoring for the moment the parameters $d$, $d_c$ etc (and noting that this author has used $k^2$ in place of our $\rho_k$). We need the minimum value of $h$ to be negative to ensure an instability. To compute this condition, we will make an important approximation -- namely that we can treat $h(\rho_k)$ as a function of a \emph{continuous} variable, $\rho_k$, despite the fact that really $\rho_k$ can only take a discrete set of values. For domains which are sufficiently large, this approximation is OK, as the modes will scale like $1/L^2$ (see, for example \cref{mode_numbers}).

With this approximation in mind, and since we know that $h$ has a single global minimum, we compute the wave number at this minimum as:
$$
h'(\rho_k^*) = 2D\rho_k^*  -  (G_v+ D F_u)=0 \implies \rho_k^ = \frac{G_v+ D F_u}{2D},
$$
and so we have that the minimum value of $h$ is:
\begin{equation}
    h(\rho_k^*) =  \frac{(G_v+ D F_u)^2}{4D} -  \frac{(G_v+ D F_u)^2}{2D}+ (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) = -\frac{(G_v+ D F_u)^2}{4D} + (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v).
\end{equation}
Rearranging this, our condition for instability (that $h(\rho_k^*)<0$) is equivalent to:
\begin{equation}
    (G_v+ D F_u)^2 - 4D(F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) > 0.
\end{equation}

\section{Biological implications of the Turing conditions}
The summary of the previous analysis is as follows. 
\begin{info}
All of the following inequalities are \emph{necessary} conditions for a diffusion-driven instability (that is, the growth rates of perturbations to a spatially homogeneous equilibrium satisfy $\Re(\lambda_0)<0$ but $\Re(\lambda_\v{k})>0$ for some wave vector $\v{k}$):
\begin{align}
\label{turingconditions} F_u +G_v <0, \quad F_uG_v  -G_uF_v> 0,\\
\nonumber G_v + DF_u>0,\quad (G_v+ D F_u)^2 - 4 D (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) >0.
\end{align}
These four conditions are the so-called \emph{Turing conditions}.
\end{info}
These conditions are necessary and sufficient for this kind of instability in the case of very large domains, as we have made one assumption in deriving them. Namely, for a fixed bounded domain, $\rho_k$ will be a discrete set of numbers, and none of these may fall within the range of unstable wave numbers. 

It is also worth mentioning that, while this analysis is a bit technical, the only thing we have learned is that a spatially homogeneous equilibrium is unstable to spatial perturbations. What happens after such an instability? Well in general this situation can be quite complex, but most of the time in practice these instabilities lead to pitchfork-like inhomogeneous steady states emerging from the spatially homogeneous equilibrium. The schematic in \cref{PitchforkTuring} illustrates how branches of spatially varying states can emerge from a homogeneous state. Such a `pitchfork' structure is indeed found in this kind of Turing instability, though the full nonlinear analysis of the bifurcation past the linear instability is beyond the scope of this course. Still, even the linear analysis provides enormous insight into the kinds of biological systems which can exhibit this sort of diffusion-driven patterning.

We now use the four conditions given in \cref{turingconditions} to make some important observations on the kinds of systems for which this instability is possible. This is a crucial step, and really allows us to say something meaningful biologically using these relatively simple inequalities.

\subsection{The Turing instability requires short-range activation, long-range inhibition}

The first and third condition imply that $F_u$ and $G_v$ must be of opposing sign. To see this we note that if they had the same sign then $F_u + G_v<0$ tells us they would have to be negative, but then the third condition would say
\begin{equation}
D \vert F_u \vert + \vert G_v\vert < 0 \implies D <-\frac{\vert G_v\vert}{\vert F_u\vert}.
\end{equation} 
But physically we need $D>0$ (negative diffusion does not make sense) so this cannot be permissible. Therefore, if $F_u>0$ we must have $G_v<0$ and vice versa.

One can see that this condition (of opposing $F_u$ and $G_v$) make physical sense. The `reaction' $F$ promotes $u$ and $G$ promotes $v$. Consider for example the case $F_u>0$ and $G_v<0$. To induce a separation in concentration peaks, we want it to be the case that where a small increase in  $u$  leads to an acceleration in the production of $u$ to coincide with small increases in $v$ leading to a drop in $v$ so that (in this case) $u$ can become dominant over $v$. Decreases in $u$ and $v$ should lead to $v$ being favoured. If both  $F_u$ and $G_v$ are the same sign they will both tend to grow and decay simultaneously, this will not promote separation and hence pattern formation. 

The biochemical terminology here is that the $u$ species is a self-activator (assuming without loss of generality that $F_u>0$), and that $v$ is a self-inhibitor. Often this is shortened to `activator and inhibitor' dynamics, though it is important to note that we have only determined how these species interact with themselves (and only at the steady state $(u_0,v_0)$).

What does this requirement say about the permissible values of $D$? Let's fix $F_u>0$ so that $u$ is the activator, so we must have $G_v<0$.  We then have from the first and the third inequalities in \cref{turingconditions}:
\begin{equation}
    F_u + G_v<0 \iff F_u < -G_v \implies \vert F_u \vert < \vert G_v \vert \implies \frac{\vert G_v\vert }{\vert F_u \vert} >1,
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
DF_u+G_v > 0 \implies D\vert F_u\vert +\vert G_v\vert >0 \implies D>\frac{\vert G_v\vert }{\vert F_u \vert} >1.
\end{equation} 
  A similar argument shows that if $G_v>0$, we must have $F_u<0$ and $D<1$. Note that these inequalities are strict, i.e.\ $D$ cannot equal $1$. This is why the Turing instability is often referred to as a diffusion-driven instability, as it requires that the diffusion rates of the two species are different. We can also see that the inhibitor in either case must always diffuse more quickly than the activator. This is referred to as short-range activation, long-range inhibition, or sometimes as LALI: local activation, lateral inhibition. 
 
 
 
 \subsection{Restrictions on sign structures of the Jacobian}
 \begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{images2/JacobianDiagram}
    \caption{An image from Murray's \emph{Mathematical Biology} vol.\ 2, chapter 2, demonstrating the two permissible sign structures of the Jacobian, and what they correspond to both in terms of unstable linear patterns, and in terms of their phase planes. Panels (a) and (b) are diagrams of the signalling kinetics, panels (c) and (d) are in-phase and out-of-phase patterns respectively due to these off-diagonal terms, and panels (e) and (f) are phase portraits near the equilibrium, including the nullclines and the flow around the equilibrium.}
    \label{JacobianSignsFig}
\end{figure}

What about the other terms in the Jacobian, $F_v$ and $G_u$? Well since $F_u$ and $G_v$ have opposite signs, we must have $F_uG_v<0$. But the second condition means that we must have $F_uG_v  -G_uF_v> 0$, so this inequality can never be satisfied unless $F_v$ and $G_u$ have opposite signs. Therefore, the Jacobian matrix must take one of the following forms:
$$ \m{J} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
+ & -\\
+ & -
\end{pmatrix},\quad  \begin{pmatrix}
+ & +\\
- & -
\end{pmatrix},\quad  \begin{pmatrix}
- & -\\
+ & +
\end{pmatrix},\quad  \begin{pmatrix}
- & +\\
- & +
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Of course, one can always just consider the first two as the latter two are just a relabelling of $u$ and $v$ in a sense, whereas the first two have qualitatively different behaviours. In particular, you can show that the coefficient of the linear term of $u_1$ and $v_1$ will be in-phase or out-of-phase, depending on which of these two forms it takes. While $u_1$ and $v_1$ will have different forms (and especially the functions satisfying the nonlinear reaction--diffusion equations, $u$ and $v$, will generally appear very different from one another), the maxima and minima of their concentrations will coincide or be exactly out-of-phase with one another. 

To see this in and out of phase behaviour, consider the second equation in \cref{turinglin}:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k v_1 = G_u u_1 + G_v v_1 - \rho_k D v_1.
\end{equation} Let's also assume we have $F_u>0$ and $G_v<0$ (so that $D>1$ for a pattern-forming instability). Moving the $v_1$ terms to the left side, we have:
$$
(\lambda_\v{k} - G_v + D \rho_k)v_1 = G_u u_1.
$$
For an instability, we must have $\lambda_\v{k}>0$, and by assumption the other two terms on the left are positive, so $v_1$ and $u_1$ are related in sign by $G_u$. That is, if $G_u>0$, then $u_1$ and $v_1$ must be of the same sign, and hence their spatial oscillations must be in-phase. If $G_u<0$, they must be out-of-phase, as there is now a sign difference between them, so anywhere $u_1$ is positive, $v_1$ must be negative and vice-versa. See \cref{JacobianSignsFig}.

This has useful biological applications, as often an experimentalist has identified a candidate activator and inhibitor, and knows if they have peaks (that is, regions of high concentration) in the same place with one another, or in different places. This can help determine the correct form for a model of their system, as often we do not understand the molecular biology well enough to write down what forms $F$ and $G$ should take. Similar remarks can be made about ecological applications, particularly predator--prey interactions.

\end{extra-reading}



\section{A summary of the Turing instability result in one and two dimensions}
Just for a concrete implementation of the above, let's consider the case of one and two spatial dimensions, given below as 1-D and 2-D respectively. Consider a system 
\begin{align}
\label{tsys}
\pd{u}{t} &= \nabla^2u +  F(u,v),\\
\pd{v}{t} &= D\nabla^2v +  G(u,v).
\end{align}
on a domain in 1-D $[0,L]$, or in 2-D $[0,L_1]\times[0,L_2]$ with coordinate systems (1-D) $x$ or (2-D) ($x_1,x_2$).

We consider this system subject to no-flux boundary conditions in 1-D:
\begin{align}
\pd{u}{x}(0,t) = \pd{u}{x}(L,t) = 0,\\
\pd{v}{x}(0,t) = \pd{v}{x}(L,t) = 0;
\end{align}
or in 2-D:
\begin{align}
\pd{u}{x_1}(0,x_2,t) = \pd{u}{x_1}(L_1,x_2,t) =\pd{u}{x_2}(x_1,0,t)=\pd{u}{x_2}(x_1,L_2,t) = 0,\\
\pd{v}{x_1}(0,x_2,t) = \pd{v}{x_1}(L_1,x_2,t) =\pd{v}{x_2}(x_1,0,t)=\pd{v}{x_2}(x_1,L_2,t) = 0.
\end{align}
In all cases we assume the system has a \emph{homogenous} equilibrium 
\begin{equation}
F(u_0,v_0) =0,\quad G(u_0,v_0) =0.
\end{equation}
If the system satisfies the following set of inequalities (these involve the elements of the Jacobian evaluated at the homogeneous equilibrium): 
\begin{align}
F_u +G_v <0, \quad F_uG_v  -G_uF_v> 0,\\
\nonumber G_v + DF_u>0,\quad (G_v+ D F_u)^2 - 4 D (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) >0,
\end{align}
then the homogeneous steady state is unstable, allowing inhomogeneous patterns to form, as long as the domain is sufficiently large. The inhomogeneities of the morphogen, $u_1(\v{x},t)$, which are solutions to the  linearised version of \cref{tsys}, will take the form (in 1-D):\footnote{The summation index is not important, but I have changed it to $n$ in this subsection from $k$ to be consistent with the 2-D results.}
\begin{equation}
u_1(x,t) =\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}A_{n}\e^{\lambda_n t}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),
\end{equation} 
or (in 2-D):
\begin{equation}
u_1(x_1,x_2,t) =\sum_{n_1=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n_2=0}^{\infty}A_{(n_1,n_2)}\e^{\lambda_{(n_1,n_2)} t}\cos\left(\frac{n_1 \pi x_1}{L_1}\right)\cos\left(\frac{n_2\pi x_2}{L_2} \right).
\end{equation} 

We write the spatial eigenvalues in 1-D as:
\begin{equation}\label{1-D_eigs}
\rho_n = \pi^2\left(\frac{n^2}{L^2}\right),
\end{equation}
and in 2-D as,
\begin{equation}\label{2-D_eigs}
\rho_{(n_1,n_2)} = \pi^2\left(\frac{n_1^2}{L_1^2} + \frac{n_2^2}{L_2^2}\right).
\end{equation}

The conditions 
\begin{equation}
F_u +G_v <0, \quad F_uG_v  -G_uF_v> 0,
\end{equation}
ensure the homogeneous $n_1=n_2=0$ part of the solution decays exponentially.

The conditions 
\begin{equation}
G_v + DF_u>0,\quad (G_v+ D F_u)^2 - 4 D (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v) >0
\end{equation}
ensure that we can solve (in 1-D)
\begin{equation}
 D\rho_n^2  -  \rho_n(G_v+ D F_u)+ (F_uG_v  -G_uF_v)= 0,
\end{equation}
in order the find a \emph{real} valued domain $\rho_n \in[\rho_n^{\min},\rho_n^{\max}]$ which defines the set of possible patterns which will grow in time, that is $\lambda_{(n)}>0$. In 2-D, this is exactly the same except that we replace the index $n$ with $(n_1, n_2)$, and for any given value of $\rho_{(n_1, n_2)}$, there can be different values of $n_1$ and $n_2$ that give rise to this value. 


In either case, the pattern is assumed to be observed when the morphogen rises above some concentration value $a$ ($u_1>a$) to stimulate some chemical/physical process. In the context of a developing embryo, such a region of high concentration will lead some cells to differentiating (turning on or off some genes to express a certain `phenotype' or set of behaviour), which will lead to subsequent restructuring of the tissue locally etc. When this happens for some periodic pattern, this can lead to things like hair follicles expressing different colours, or the digits forming in one's hand etc -- at least this is the theory! 

\section{Geometry and discrete wavenumber selection}
The four algebraic Turing conditions are necessary on a finite domain. They become sufficient for the linear instability only when an admissible spatial eigenvalue lies in the unstable interval; sufficiently large domains permit this. The derivation of the growth rates, with solutions given by \cref{turinglambda}, is instead a general result that depends on the spatial eigenvalues of the Laplacian $\rho_k$. It is these spatial eigenvalues which encode both the geometry of the domain (its lengths etc), as well as the boundary conditions, as we saw in the case of Neumann and Dirichlet conditions in the last chapter. Here we discuss the case of finite mode selection briefly, just to get across some intuition of how this works on a fixed bounded domain. We will also continue to restrict to the case of Neumann boundary conditions.

We saw that all growth rates will have negative real part unless $\det(\m{M})=h(\rho_k)$, given by \cref{h_eq}, was negative. Finding the zeroes of this equation, assuming parameters where the minimum is negative, allows us to find an interval of unstable wavenumbers. This interval will be exactly where we expect a positive growth rate, as $h(\rho_k)<0$ there. See the curve given by $d>d_c$ in \cref{disp_rel_fig}, and note that the band indicated in (b) is exactly the same region for which $h$ is negative in (a). 


The parameters determine an open interval $\rho_-<\rho_k<\rho_+$ of unstable spatial eigenvalues. On a finite domain, at least one admissible eigenvalue must lie strictly inside this interval. In one dimension $q_n=n\pi/L$ has spacing $\pi/L$, whereas $\rho_n=q_n^2$ has spacing $(2n+1)\pi^2/L^2$. Increasing $L$ makes the spectrum denser within a fixed positive interval. Solving $h(\rho)=0$ gives the endpoints:
\begin{align}
\label{ksbounds}
\ks^{\pm}= \frac{1}{2D}\left[(DF_u + G_v) \pm \sqrt{(D F_u +G_v)^2 -4D(F_uG_v - F_vG_u)}\right].
\end{align}

In higher dimensions, the size (the $L_i$) of the individual domain directions has a relationship between a given $k_i$ and the relevant mode $n_i$, i.e.\
\begin{equation}
k_i = \frac{n_i\pi}{L_i}.
\end{equation}
For a fixed $n_i$ the value of $k_i$ decreases as the domain size $L_i$ increases. We see that the total spatial eigenvalue, in two dimensions given by \cref{2-D_eigs} and in the general case by \cref{mode_numbers}, thus scales with each $L_i$ individually. Thus, the number of modes in between $\ks\in[\ks^{-},\ks^{+}]$ will increase as the length of the domain increases. The effect of the parameter $D$ is a little more complex.

  If we  send $D \to \infty$ then the permissible bounds of $\ks$ will tend to $\ks\in(0, F_u)$ (consider $\ks^\mp$ and look at where $D$ is). This is sometimes a useful limit to consider. 


\subsection{The peak unstable mode}

For a fixed wavenumber, Equation \cref{turinglambda} gives the growth rate $\lambda(\ks)$. If we assume that the domain is sufficiently large, we can compute a value of $\ks$ for which $\lambda(\ks)$ is maximal. This will then be the mode which grows the fastest, at least as long as the linear approximation is valid. To find this we differentiate $\lambda(\ks)$ with respect to $\ks$ and solve for $\fd{\lambda}{\ks}=0$. Since $\lambda(\ks)$ takes the form
\begin{align}
&\frac{1}{2}\left[(F_u +G_v) -\ks(D+1) + \left\{\left((F_u +G_v) -\ks(D+1) \right)^2- 4\det(\m{M})\right\}^{1/2}\right],\\
&\det(\m{M}) = D\ks^2 -  \ks(DF_u +G_v) + (F_uG_v -F_vG_u).
\end{align}
This is not a trivial task! With no little effort solving $\fd{\lambda}{\ks}=0$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{kmaximal}
\ks^* = \frac{1}{D-1}\left[(D+1)\left(\frac{-F_v G_u}{D}\right)^{1/2}-F_u+G_v\right],
\end{equation}
as the fastest growing mode. Alongside bounding the range of unstable modes, this computation often matches observed patterns from full numerical simulations reasonably well, and so despite the algebraic difficulty, it can be a useful heuristic for predicting the wavelength between pattern elements (e.g.~the spacing between spots) given model parameters.

\section{Example: Turing analysis of the Schnakenberg system}\label{Schnack_Sect}
Consider the reaction--diffusion system
    \begin{align}\label{RD_System_Schnack}
\pd{u}{t} &= \nabla^2u +  F(u,v),\\
\pd{v}{t} &= D\nabla^2v +  G(u,v).
\end{align}
with the reaction kinetics,
\begin{equation}
F = a+u^2v- u,\quad G = b- u^2v,
\end{equation}
with $a$, $b$ both positive parameters. These are known as the Schnakenberg kinetics, or alternatively as the substrate-depleted kinetics. These reaction terms are those we used in looking at switching oscillatory behaviour in last terms notes, there called the enzyme-reaction system. Here we will go through the steps of finding parameter regimes for which this system exhibits diffusion-driven instability.

\subsection*{Find the homogeneous equilibrium}
The equilibrium can be found readily by adding $F+G$ to find $u_0$, and then substituting this into $G=0$ (or $F=0$) to find $v_0$. We obtain,
\begin{equation}
u_0 = a+b,\quad v_0 = \frac{b}{(a+b)^2}.
\end{equation}
so $a+b> 0$ and $b\geq 0$ for physical solutions (and in realistic biological situations, we also tend to require $a>0$).

\subsection*{Compute the Jacobian}
The beauty of the Turing analysis is that we have already done this in the general case (see \cref{turinglin}). We simply need to find the components of the Jacobian evaluated at the steady state. These are given by:
\begin{align}
\label{enzymepds}
F_u &= 2u_0v_0-1=\frac{b-a}{a+b}, \quad F_v =(u_0)^2= (a+b)^2,\\ \nonumber G_u &=-2u_0 v_0= \frac{-2b}{a+b},\quad G_v = -(u_0)^2= -(a+b)^2.
\end{align}


\subsection*{Apply the Turing conditions}
As we have already solved the linear reaction--diffusion system, we can just make use of the Turing conditions. So $F_v>0$ so that $v$ is an inhibitor. We see that the cross terms also have the right sign structure for out-of-phase patterns, as $F_v>0$ and $G_u<0$. Since we must then have $F_u>0$ (so that $u$ is an activator), we require $b>a$.

As $F_u$ and $G_v$ must be of opposing sign $F_u>0$ so $b>a$. Further $D>1$ as $v$ is the inhibitor.  

\subsubsection{Condition 1}
Firstly we require $\tr(\m{J})=F_u+G_v<0$ which says,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{b-a}{a+b}-(a+b)^2 <0 \implies (a+b)^3>b-a.
\end{equation}
We will also need $b-a>0$ from requiring $F_u>0$, though the following two conditions below will implicitly require this (and we do not need this for the homogeneous equilibrium to be stable in the absence of diffusion).

\subsubsection{Condition 2}
Next we need $\det(\m{J})>0$ which implies
\begin{equation}
    -\frac{b-a}{a+b}(a+b)^2+2b(a+b) = (a+b)^2>0,
\end{equation}
which is trivially satisfied.

\subsubsection{Condition 3}
Now we need $DF_u+G_v>0$ which says,
\begin{equation}
    D\frac{b-a}{a+b}-(a+b)^2 >0 \implies (a+b)^3<D(b-a).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Condition 4}
Finally we have to check the last condition, which is often much more algebraically involved than the other three. We need $(DF_u+G_v)^2-4D(F_uG+_v-F_vG_u)>0$ which says,
\begin{equation}
    \left(D\frac{b-a}{a+b}-(a+b)^2\right)^2-4D(a+b)^2 = \frac{1}{(a+b)^2}\left (D(b-a)-(a+b)^3\right)^2-4D(a+b)^2>0,
\end{equation}
which can be simplified as,
\begin{equation}
    (D(b-a)-(a+b)^3)^2>4D(a+b)^4.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Summary of the conditions}
We can summarise the conditions by saying that for the homogeneous steady state to be stable, we simply require,
\begin{equation}\label{hom_ss_stab_schnack}
    (a+b)^3>b-a,
\end{equation}
and that for a diffusion-driven instability, we also need,
\begin{equation}
    (a+b)^3<D(b-a), \quad \textrm{and} \quad (D(b-a)-(a+b)^3)^2>4D(a+b)^4.
\end{equation}
As noted, the requirement that $b>a$ for $F_u$ to be positive can be seen in the first of these two conditions. We also see that, as long as $b-a>0$, both of these conditions will be automatically satisfied in the limit of $D \to \infty$. We can confirm this by plotting the conditions numerically.

This is a major obstacle of robustness of this theory, as we would really want a large and robust parameter space where we see these instabilities, which is not very sensitive to small random fluctuations.




\subsection{Mode selection via parameter controls (non-examinable)}
We can also consider a  means by which we exert control on the patterns formed by this system, as long as we can control the kinetic parameters (and thereby the Jacobian). Such control approaches have been used in chemical applications of Turing systems, as well as in more modern synthetic biology applications. 

The Turing conditions tell us that when 
\begin{equation}
(DF_u +G_v)^2 = 4D(F_uG_v-F_vG_u),
\end{equation}
 the system is on the border of allowing turning instabilities. This occurs when 
\begin{equation}
\ks =  \frac{D F_u + G_v}{2D}.
\end{equation}
So only one mode can potentially be unstable if we are just passed this threshold. One could for example demand this occur for an $n_1=5,n_2=5$ mode in a 2-D system (creating a checkerboard pattern like in  \cref{cartpat}(a)). 
In the example we are looking at 
\begin{equation}
\ks = \frac{D(b-a)-(a+b)^3}{2D(a+b)} = 25\pi^2\left(\frac{1}{L_1^2} +\frac{1}{L_2^2} \right),
\end{equation}
which would, in principle, allow us to pick parameters such that the system is unstable exactly at that mode. In fact the amplitude of the mode can, to some degree, also be controlled, though this is beyond our scope here. In fact recent efforts have allowed for the design of elaborate Turing patterns controlled via spatial heterogeneity and nonlinear spot and stripe selection mechanisms, though again you will not be expected to be familiar with these. See \href{https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11538-021-00870-y}{this paper} for further discussion. An example is \cref{TuringHet}.

\section{Usefulness \& limitations of Turing-type analysis}
Recall Mathematical Methods \S2.6: nonlinear terms generally couple spatial modes. Its non-examinable \S4.7 gives further examples. The independent modal growth rates derived here therefore predict the onset of instability, while saturation, competition between modes and the final pattern require nonlinear information.

The critical  point to make regarding the Turing analysis is that it is a linear analysis. Essentially all of the interesting systems we shall consider (and indeed not consider) are nonlinear; nature is cruel like that! However this linear analysis tells us something about the permissible pattern (the unstable modes) which the system can take. In actual fact as relayed in section 2.4 of Murray, this constraint tends to be quite accurate. That is to say when the full nonlinear system is solved numerically it tends to be the case that the pattern belongs to the permissible space indicted by the Turing analysis. This can be formally justified near the onset of instability (that is, near the boundaries of the Turing space), though nonlinearity can play an important role in modifying the exact nature of the patterns presented. Of course the Turing analysis only presents us with a range of possible patterns, and especially in two and three dimensions, there can often be many. Indeed one can obtain many different patterned states from full numerical simulations of reaction--diffusion systems, and this kind of `multistability' becomes exponentially difficult to understand in larger domains.

Of course Turing's idea was that his analysis captures the system's behaviour just as it develops the pattern. In this case some parameter of the system is  increased pushing the system just past the point of stability. We can choose the $\varepsilon$ in the linearisation to be the difference this parameter has from the loss of equilibrium. In this case the linear approximation will likely be a much more reliable indicator of the system's behaviour. In this way we can relate a Turing instability at the onset of an instability to the pitchfork bifurcation studied in  the term-one discussion of bifurcations; see \cref{PitchforkTuring}. Still, one can greatly improve an analytical prediction by using more sophisticated analytical tools, such as weakly nonlinear analysis, or the more rigorous study of `shadow-limit' systems which essentially formalise the limit $D \to \infty$ more carefully.

\section{Summary \& relaxing the assumptions}
The fundamental components of a Turing analysis are 
\begin{enumerate}
\item{The loss of stability of a homogeneously mixed system of biochemicals/populations/cells leading to the formation of inhomogeneous patterns.}
\item{From the point of view of the stability analysis, the crucial assumptions were the stability of  the zero mode (stability of the homogeneous equilibrium in the absence of diffusion) and the instability of some  inhomogeneous $\ks\neq 0$ modes which formed the pattern.}
\item{The critical result of these assumptions was that a finite range of $\ks$ values (specific patterns). 
This is a plausible explanation of why we often see spots and stripes, patterns which would be the consequence of only a subset of modes being promoted.  }
\end{enumerate}
A number of the aspects of the analysis are not fundamental and can be relaxed:
\begin{enumerate}
\item{The boundary conditions do not necessarily need to be no-flux, which forbids anything leaving or entering the system. Similarly periodic boundary conditions, which are more realistic for some animal skin patterns, can give a similar result and comparable patterns. However, as in the case of a harsh environment in the previous chapter, the pattern formation paradigm is not the same for Dirichlet boundary conditions. This is because the zero mode is not permissible so does not need to be suppressed. This can mean the set of growing modes is more difficult to analyse.}
\item{The reaction--diffusion system is the sum of linear diffusion terms representing spreading and reaction terms. Pattern formation can and does occur for nonlinear diffusion, but often the analysis is more complex. }
\item{The domain will not generally be Cartesian. In fact, whilst it is an excellent mathematical starting point, it is often the case the domain should  have more complex geometry (think animal appendages). As we shall see in the next chapter, different domain geometries do not typically affect the Turing conditions themselves, but change the mathematical form of the patterns formed and for small domains influence $\rho_k$.}
\item{We assumed the instability occurred for  a homogeneous equilibrium. In fact there is a lot of interest in pattern formation around heterogeneous states in space and time, though the analysis of such systems requires more sophisticated mathematical tools than those we have developed here.}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Three-dimensional patterns}\label{3D_Patterns}

While a lot of the patterns we immediately think of in biology are two-dimensional (spots and stripes on animal coats, for example), many biological structures are inherently three-dimensional. Even animal coat patterns are induces by hair follicles, which are the buds in a developing embryo that become the roots of hairs, and these inherently have a three-dimensional structure. Other examples are the differentiation of different cell types to form different organs and tissues inside a body, leading to a wide array of complex spatial structures that have developed differently. 

In this section we will consider the simplest case of three spatial dimensions by looking at eigenfunctions of the Laplacian in a cuboid or `box-like' domain. In the first row of \cref{3D}, we give examples of patterns in such a domain corresponding to simulations of the Schnakenberg system studied in \cref{Schnack_Sect}. The second row shows patterns in a cylindrical domain which we will discuss in subsequent sections. Unlike two-dimensional spatial patterns, which tend towards either spot-like structures or stripe-like structures, three-dimensional patterns have a variety of different forms, and there is often no simple classification of them (though there are analogues of `spots' and `stripes' in the form of sphere-like solutions and lamellae-like solutions). Note, however, that these three-dimensional structures are at least qualitatively similar independent of the geometry. We will come back to this point at the end of the chapter.

\begin{equation}\label{Helm3D}
w_{n_x,n_y,n_z}=\cos\left(\frac{n_x\pi x}{L_x}\right)
\cos\left(\frac{n_y\pi y}{L_y}\right)
\cos\left(\frac{n_z\pi z}{L_z}\right),\qquad n_x,n_y,n_z\geq0,
\end{equation}

\rho_{n_x,n_y,n_z}=\left(\frac{n_x\pi}{L_x}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{n_y\pi}{L_y}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n_z\pi}{L_z}\right)^2.
\end{equation}

Changing one side length changes the available eigenvalues and the physical spacing in that direction. Different modes can also have the same eigenvalue. In a cube, for example, $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$ share one eigenvalue and hence the same pair of temporal growth rates, despite their different orientations. Linear theory predicts the growth of their amplitudes but does not select a unique final orientation or nonlinear pattern. Initial perturbations and subsequent nonlinear interactions matter.

The simulations in \cref{3D} illustrate the variety of three-dimensional structures. Their comparison with the eigenmodes should be used to interpret possible spatial scales and symmetries, rather than to identify a nonlinear solution with one eigenfunction.

    \caption{A diagram of \emph{E. coli} in a petri dish. Typically they grow in a single large colony called a biofilm which is well-approximated as a two-dimensional structure. If food is scarce, these colonies can break apart as in the right panel.}
    \label{Petri}
\end{figure}

We model this using a two-dimensional reaction--diffusion equation,
\begin{equation}
    \pd{u}{t} = D\nabla^2 u + u(1-u),
\end{equation}
where $D$ is a scaled diffusion coefficient. We have nondimensionalised the bacterial density $u$ and timescale $t$ such that the carrying capacity of the culture medium (the bacteria's food source) is $1$, and that the growth rate is also $1$. Our domain is the two-dimensional disc $(r,\theta)$ with $r\in [0, L_r]$ the radial coordinate, and $\theta\in [0, 2\pi)$ the angular coordinate. In this polar coordinate system the Laplacian takes the form
\begin{equation}
\nabla^2u  = \frac{1}{r}\pd{}{r}\left(r \pd{u}{r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\pdd{u}{\theta}.
\end{equation}
We have the boundary conditions,
\begin{equation}
    {u}(t,L_r,\theta) = 0,\,\, {u}(t,r,0) ={u}(t,r,2\pi),\,\,  \pd{u}{\theta}(t,r,0) = \pd{u}{\theta}(t,r,2\pi).
\end{equation}
The first of these represents bacterial death at the walls, and the second two are periodic conditions conditions in $\theta$.

\subsection{Homogeneous equilibrium \& linear stability analysis}

Despite the geometry being different, this problem is otherwise the same as that in  \cref{harsh_environment}. While there are two spatially homogeneous equilibria in the absence of the diffusion term, given by $u_0=0$ or $1$, the latter does not satisfy the boundary condition at $r=L_r$. So we can only consider instabilities around $u_0=0$. In the absence of diffusion this steady state is unstable, though this may not be the case when diffusion is considered.

The linearisation of the equation is the same as we've seen before. Writing $u = u_0 + \varepsilon u_1 = \varepsilon u_1$ we find,
\begin{equation}\label{Petri_Linear}
    \pd{u_1}{t} = D\nabla^2 u_1 + u_1(1-2u_0) = D\nabla^2 u_1 + u_1.
\end{equation}
As before, we substitute in $u_1 = \exp(\lambda_k t)w_k(r,\theta)$ to find that the perturbation's growth rates are given by,
\begin{equation}\label{Disp_Bessel_Scalar}
    \lambda_k u_1 = -\rho_k D u_1 + u_1\implies \lambda_k = -\rho_k D + 1,
\end{equation}
where the spatial eigenvalue $\rho_k$ is determined from the equation,
\begin{equation}\label{eig_petri}
    \nabla^2w_k + \rho_k w_k  = \frac{1}{r}\pd{}{r}\left(r \pd{w_k}{r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\pdd{w_k}{\theta} + \rho_k w_k = 0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Reuse the disc spectrum from Mathematical Methods}
Mathematical Methods \S\S2.4.1--2.4.2 derives the angular periodicity, radial Bessel equation and regularity condition at the origin. With $j_{n,k}$ denoting the $k$th \emph{positive} zero of $J_n$, its result in our notation is
\begin{equation}\label{R_Bessel_BC}
\rho_{k,n}=\left(\frac{j_{n,k}}{L_r}\right)^2,\qquad
w_{k,n}=J_n\left(\frac{j_{n,k}r}{L_r}\right)
\bigl[A\cos(n\theta)+B\sin(n\theta)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $n=0,1,\ldots$ and $k=1,2,\ldots$; for $n=0$ there is only the cosine branch. The $Y_n$ solution is excluded because it is unbounded at the origin. The zero of $J_n$ at the origin for $n>0$ does not supply a Dirichlet eigenmode of the disc.

The Bessel functions in \cref{besselfuncs} are recalled for orientation; their definitions and derivation are not needed again here.

\subsection{The biological conclusion: a critical dish radius}
The spatial spectrum is unchanged from the heat problem, but the modal growth rates are now
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{k,n}=1-D\left(\frac{j_{n,k}}{L_r}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The smallest spatial eigenvalue is $(j_{0,1}/L_r)^2$, with a radially symmetric eigenfunction. Consequently extinction is linearly asymptotically stable when
\begin{equation}
L_r<\sqrt{D}\,j_{0,1},
\end{equation}
and unstable when the inequality is reversed. At equality the leading linear growth rate is zero and this calculation alone does not settle the nonlinear behaviour.

This is the circular counterpart of \cref{harsh_environment_growth}. Increasing the radius reduces diffusive loss relative to reproduction, allowing a small population to grow. Increasing $D$ raises the critical radius because bacteria reach the lethal boundary more rapidly. The first growing mode describes the early population profile, not its final nonlinear amplitude. We have obtained these biological conclusions by reusing the Methods eigenvalues; the coefficients of a full Fourier--Bessel expansion are unnecessary for this stability question.

\section{Modelling whorl patterns in \emph{Acetabularia}}\label{aceta}
We now use the same modal framework to model the growth of hairs in a whorl around \emph{Acetabularia}. The new mathematics concerns an annulus with no-flux boundaries; the central biological aim is to relate hair spacing and the range of pattern formation to geometry and calcium supply. 
\begin{extra-reading}
We will follow the ideas in \textsc{Murray}, vol.~II, \S3.4. Take a look there for further details and references to the mathematical and biological literature.
\end{extra-reading}

\begin{figure}
\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=8cm]{images2/acetabularia1.jpg}}\quad\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=8cm]{images2/acetabularia2.png}}
\caption{\label{acet2}Acetabularia figures. Panel (a) a picture of Acetabularia. Panel (b) a schematic depicting the growth process.}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=10cm]{images2/polarCoords.png}} 
\end{center}
\caption{\label{pcs}Model set-up for the Acetabularia problem.}
\end{figure}
\emph{Acetabularia} is a genus of green marine algae, wherein all species are unicellular organisms, most of which are able to regenerate (see \cref{acet2}(a) for an example of the species \emph{Acetabularia ryukyuensis}). These organisms have been the subject of numerous lab experiments designed to quantitatively asses this growth process. Of particular interest in this chapter is the fact that the growth of the whorl hairs, which eventually lead to the formation of the cap (see \cref{acet2}(b)), can be shown to be mediated by the presence of calcium in the surrounding fluid. The stalk is hollow and has an annular cross-section (\cref{pcs}). The Calcium enters through the outer wall, the inner wall is impermeable.  By amputating the stalk and then charting its re-growth in the presence of various levels of calcium the following experimental findings were established:

\begin{enumerate}
\item{The number of hairs produced  is proportional to the radius of the (hollow) stalk (assuming all other experimental conditions are fixed). Hence, there is a fixed wavelength between successive hairs, consistent with a Turing-type mechanism.}
\item{The growth of the tip is seen to only occur for a finite range of calcium concentrations in the surrounding fluid, i.e.\  growth is halted if the levels are too high or too low (see  \cref{Acetabularia_Comparison_Plot}(a)).}
\end{enumerate}
 So we have the spontaneous formation of a pattern (the hairs are evenly spaced around the axis of the stalk). Further it only occurs in limited scenarios in the presence of some tune-able parameter (calcium concentration). This suggests we could model it via the Turing mechanism, with calcium playing the role of $v$, and $u$ representing a morphogen triggering hair growth.\footnote{Of course this is a vast simplification of a complex process, and mechanical aspects play key roles as well. See \href{https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2000.0565}{this paper} for an overview of the evidence for different mechanisms at different stages of development.} This proposed model was originally developed in 1985 at a meeting on the subject, and can explain the two key phenomena listed above. 
 
 We consider our domain to be the annulus, with polar coordinates $r \in [R_i, R_o]$ and $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. We assume the interaction equations take the following form
\begin{align}\label{Acetabularia_Model}
\pd{u}{t} &= \nabla^2 u+  (a-u+u^2v),\\
\nonumber\pd{v}{t} &=  D\nabla^2v+(b-u^2v).
\end{align}
This is the Schnakenberg reaction system we considered in \cref{Schnack_Sect}, but now in a polar coordinate system. We consider an annular domain with a radial coordinate system $(r,\theta)$ for which the inner and outer radii are $R_i$ and $R_o$, respectively. The inner boundary's impermeability is modelled by applying no-flux (Neumann) conditions there, i.e.\
\begin{equation}
\left.\pd{u}{r}\right|_{r= R_i} = \left.\pd{v}{r}\right|_{r= R_i} = 0. 
\end{equation}
Realistically we should allow flux of the calcium $\v{v}$ on the outer boundary. As an approximation (as inhomogeneous boundary conditions make the problem substantially harder), we note that the annular width is small compared to the radius of cross-section, so we model the calcium source through the term $b$ and use zero flux conditions on the outer boundary as well. These are given by
\begin{equation}
\left.\pd{u}{r}\right|_{r= R_o} = \left.\pd{v}{r}\right|_{r= R_o} = 0. 
\end{equation}
The parameter $a$ will represent a basal (that is, in the background and not part of our model) production of the morphogen $u$. As in the last chapter, we have nondimensionalised the timescale such that the parameter $D$ represents the ratio of the two diffusion coefficients.

\subsection{Reuse the kinetics; change the spatial spectrum}
The equilibrium and reaction Jacobian are already known from \cref{Schnack_Sect}:
\[
u_0=a+b,\qquad v_0=\frac{b}{(a+b)^2},\qquad
J=\mat{2u_0v_0-1}{u_0^2}{-2u_0v_0}{-u_0^2}.
\]
The equilibrium satisfies the no-flux boundary conditions. For each annular spatial eigenfunction the temporal rates are therefore the eigenvalues of
\begin{equation}\label{linearised}
M_k=J-\rho_k\operatorname{diag}(1,D),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}\label{eigeneq}
-\nabla^2w_k=\rho_kw_k,\qquad
\partial_rw_k(R_i,\theta)=\partial_rw_k(R_o,\theta)=0.
\end{equation}
The Turing inequalities and dispersion relation are unchanged. We must determine which values of $\rho_k$ the annulus permits.

Recall the polar separation in Mathematical Methods \S2.4.1. Its angular factors $\cos(n\theta)$ and $\sin(n\theta)$ still apply. Two radial details change from the disc in \cref{Petri_Sect}: the origin is excluded, so $Y_n$ is admissible, and both boundaries now impose zero radial derivative rather than a zero value.

The radial component of the eigenfunction will again be of the form,
\begin{equation}
    R_n(r) = A_{k,n}J_n(\sqrt{\rho_k}r)+B_{k,n}Y_n(\sqrt{\rho_k}r),
\end{equation}
though we no longer have $B_n = 0$, and the values for $\rho_{k,n}$ will be different as these must satisfy new boundary conditions. The equations determining these eigenvalues are,
\begin{align}\label{R_Sys_BCs}
    R_n'(R_i)&=\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}(A_{k,n}J_n'(\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}R_i)+B_{k,n}Y_n'(\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}R_i)) = 0,\\
    R_n'(R_o)&=\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}(A_{k,n}J_n'(\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}R_o)+B_{k,n}Y_n'(\sqrt{\rho_{k,n}}R_o)) = 0.
\end{align}
For $\rho>0$, a nonzero coefficient pair requires
\begin{equation}
J_n'(\sqrt{\rho}R_i)Y_n'(\sqrt{\rho}R_o)
-Y_n'(\sqrt{\rho}R_i)J_n'(\sqrt{\rho}R_o)=0.
\end{equation}
Its positive roots give the radial branches for each angular index $n$. Numerical root finding supplies them when needed. The biologically useful thin-annulus approximation below avoids solving this determinant explicitly.

For $\rho=0$ the Neumann eigenfunction is the constant function. It is best treated directly, rather than by substituting zero into a representation involving $Y_n(\sqrt{\rho}r)$. For positive eigenvalues, both Bessel functions are allowed on the annulus because the origin is excluded.

\subsection{Thin annuli: predicting the number of hairs}\label{merged:thinannulus}
An important geometric prediction can be seen without solving the full Bessel determinant. Let the annulus have mean radius $R=(R_i+R_o)/2$ and thickness $h=R_o-R_i$, with $h/R\ll1$. For the lowest radial branch with no-flux boundaries, the eigenfunction is approximately constant across the thickness. The leading spatial equation is therefore
\begin{equation}
-\frac{1}{R^2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2w}{\mathrm{d}\theta^2}=\rho w,
\qquad w(\theta+2\pi)=w(\theta).
\end{equation}
Hence $w_n=A\cos(n\theta)+B\sin(n\theta)$ and
\begin{equation}\label{merged:ringmodes}
\rho_n\simeq n^2/R^2,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.
\end{equation}
For fixed reaction parameters and diffusion coefficients, the dispersion relation selects a preferred spatial eigenvalue $\rho_*$ independently of $R$. The fastest allowed angular mode is obtained by comparing the growth rates at the discrete values $n^2/R^2$. Its mode number is approximately
\begin{equation}
n_*\simeq R\sqrt{\rho_*},\qquad
\ell_*\simeq\frac{2\pi R}{n_*}\simeq\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\rho_*}}.
\end{equation}
Thus the number of repeated features grows approximately in proportion to stalk radius, while their physical spacing remains approximately fixed. Integer mode selection produces steps rather than an exactly continuous relationship. The interpretation as hair number also assumes that one hair develops at each relevant concentration maximum.

This prediction requires fixed kinetic parameters, a thin annulus, and dominance of the lowest radial branch. Changing thickness enough to excite radial structure, changing the chemistry during growth, or nonlinear competition between modes can alter it. Figure~\ref{Acetabularia_Sims} illustrates why increasing thickness must be distinguished from increasing circumference.

\subsection{Comparison with regeneration rates}

Experimental data shown in \cref{Acetabularia_Comparison_Plot}(a) indicate that  there is a finite range of Calcium concentrations (modelled by the parameter $b$ in our model) for which full hair growth (regeneration) can occur. We also see that, within this range the hair spacing decreases as the calcium concentration increases, quickly at first then more gradually. Further still the amplitude of the pattern decreases as the concentration approaches both ends of this range.

In order for patterns to form we must enforce all the Turing conditions. We have already established for this system that, for fixed $D$, there is a range of values of $b$ which satisfy the Turing conditions. We can, with some effort, parametrise the boundaries given in  \cref{SchnakTuringSpace}, which correspond to values of $(a,b)$ precisely where some growth rate $\lambda_k=0$, and hence on one side we observe Turing instability, and on the other we observe stability of the spatially homogeneous state (or homogeneous instability, for the white region). \subsection{What does agreement with regeneration data mean?}
For fixed $a$ and $D$, changing $b$ traces a line through the Turing parameter region. If that line enters and leaves the region, the model predicts a finite window in which the homogeneous state can undergo diffusion-driven instability. On a finite annulus we must also check that an allowed eigenvalue lies in the unstable band. This offers an explanation for why regeneration might require an intermediate range of calcium concentrations.

The comparison is qualitative: the model parameter $b$ must be related to calcium concentration, and concentration variations must be related to the cellular process producing a hair. A positive growth rate measures early amplification of a perturbation; it is not a prediction of the final fraction of hairs regenerated. In particular, the plateau in the experimental regeneration curve cannot be derived from linear stability alone. A threshold response and nonlinear saturation are possible additional ingredients, which would need to be modelled or measured.

Together, the finite patterning window and the radius--hair-number relationship provide two distinct tests of the proposed mechanism. Reproducing a pattern's appearance is useful, but predicting how it changes when geometry or experimental conditions change is a stronger test.

\section{Turing analysis on general domains}
Mathematical Methods \S2.5, `Eigenvalues are more fundamental than separation', and \S4.6, `Where the analytic method reaches its limits', explain the distinction needed here. Separation of variables is one way to find spatial eigenfunctions. The modal stability argument does not require a separable domain.

For a homogeneous equilibrium, constant reaction and diffusion matrices, and common homogeneous boundary conditions admitting a Laplacian eigenbasis, the calculation remains
\[
-\nabla^2w_k=\rho_kw_k,\qquad
\boldsymbol{a}_k'=(J-\rho_k\mathcal D)\boldsymbol{a}_k.
\]
The kinetics and diffusion determine the unstable band; the domain and boundary conditions determine which eigenvalues fall in it. The eigenfunctions describe the corresponding spatial variations. Changing shape can therefore change both the availability and appearance of growing modes without changing the reaction kinetics.

Most domain shapes do not allow simple separation of variables. Their eigenvalues and eigenfunctions can instead be approximated numerically, then used in the same mode matrices. Mathematical Methods \S3.5 extends the eigenfunction viewpoint to higher dimensions. On curved surfaces the relevant operator is the surface Laplacian; on networks it is a graph Laplacian. These extensions preserve the connection between spatial spectrum and modal dynamics, while requiring an appropriate spatial operator and boundary conditions.
\end{nem}

\chapter{Generalised pattern formation in reaction--transport systems}


In the last chapter we discussed how in principle the Turing analysis in one spatial dimension captures more complex geometries in higher dimensions, subject to solving the Helmholtz equation for the spatial eigenvalues $\rho_k$. In this chapter we will generalise the analysis to systems of equations which have terms that correspond to spatial transport beyond purely Fickian diffusion. We will separate this into two sub-topics: cross-diffusion (including models of chemotaxis and other direction motion), and nonlocal spatial transport. The first will involve essentially the Laplacian and its eigenfunctions, but we will no longer consider the diffusion matrix $\m{D}$ to be diagonal. In the second we will discuss spatial operators more general than the Laplacian, particularly those of order higher than two.

As in the previous chapter, the mathematical ideas here build on all of the linear instability analysis we have done. So while things will get more technical, I hope it is at least helpful to see the same ideas once again in a slightly different context. The process is always the same since the first chapter of this term: find equilibria, linearise the model, and then solve the linear model to understand how perturbations of the equilibria grow or decay, depending on the parameters. The rest are details, which are important, but just remember the big picture of the forest if you get lost in the trees.

\section{Models of directed motion}
Fickian diffusion is based around the idea of small random movements. For chemicals, this represents random molecular movement essentially due to collisions of individual molecules\footnote{At the macroscopic scale, we can think of `heat' as essentially the number of collisions per unit of time, and hence a diffusion constant will increase with the temperature of a medium.}. For larger things we have modelled, such as predators and prey, this kind of Fickian diffusion also models a kind of random walking movement. But, despite how you might describe fellow commuters on a busy street, most organisms do not move primarily via random movements in arbitrary directions. Instead, they move according to signals internal or external to themselves, directing them in particular directions. 

The word \emph{taxis} describes the movement of an organism in response to a stimulus. One of the most important and well-studied examples is \emph{chemotaxis} where an organism follows a chemical trail by going in the direction of increasing gradients of this chemical. But many other kinds of directed motion exist, such as plants moving for optimal light absorption via \emph{phototaxis}, or bacteria moving along magnetic fields via \emph{magnetotaxis}. Other examples of directed motion include predators chasing prey (and the prey fleeing), birds flying annually to more suitable climates. 

We will first explore a model of snake skin pattern formation where cell movement has been theorised to play a role (and where some experimental evidence indicates that chemotactic movement of cells forms the skin patterns). From this analysis we will show how the same kind of approach applies to general transport systems.


\subsection{Snake skin pattern formation via chemotaxis}
\textbf{Relevant reading:} Murray book I, chapter 11.4; and book II, chapter 4.11.

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=12cm]{images2/snakes.png}
\caption{\label{snakepics}Snake skin pattern, both striped and spotted}
\end{center}
\end{figure}
Snake coats can exhibit a  wide variety of spotted and striped patterns, both along the length of the snake and those around the its cross-section. While some species exhibit almost identical markings, snakes within the same family can also exhibit  both length wise and width wise stripes, or mixtures of skin patterns; see  \cref{snakepics} for some examples.

Murray talks at some length about the biology of alligator stripe patterns in chapter 4, noting that there has been much experimental work on this mechanism. As Murray relates in section 4.11, there is some evidence that a similar mechanism could be at work in snake skin patterning. The basic assumptions of such a mechanism are: 
\begin{enumerate} 
\item{The pattern is fixed in the dermis, below the outer epidermis which is what we see. This is important as epidermal cells do not move much, whereas those in the dermis are much more motile.}
\item{Pigment producing cells in this region exhibit phenotypes leading to different coloured regions, and this pigment production is dependent on some chemical (the `morphogen') reaching a  critical concentration.}
\item{The motion and concentrations of these cells proceeds by a chemotactic mechanism. }
\end{enumerate}
Given these assumptions, we now write down and analyse a model of chemotaxis-driven pattern formation.

\subsection*{The model}
We let $\tilde{n}$ denote the density of pigment producing cells and $\tilde{c}$ the concentration of a chemoattractant. We can model such a scenario by the following reaction--diffusion-type system:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{unscaledsnake}
\pd{\tilde{n}}{\tilde{t}} &= D_{\tilde{n}}\nabla^2\tilde{n} -\vnabla\cdot\left(g(\tilde{n},\tilde{c})\vnabla \tilde{c}\right) + f(\tilde{n}),\\
\pd{\tilde{c}}{\tilde{t}} &= D_{\tilde{c}}\nabla^2\tilde{c} + h(\tilde{n},\tilde{c}) -  \gamma \tilde{c}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The diffusion terms and coefficients have their usual meaning, whereas the new term involving the divergence of $g(\tilde{n},\tilde{c})\vnabla \tilde{c}$ represents a directed motion towards increasing values of $\tilde{c}$ at a rate proportional to $g(\tilde{n},\tilde{c})$. The function $f$ represents cell population dynamics (i.e.~growth and death of the cells),  $h$ models the production of the chemoattractant $\tilde{c}$, and the term $-\gamma \tilde{c}$ is the natural degradation of the chemoattractant. All parameters are considered positive.

The new advection term can be interpreted as a bias in the flux of cells. Recalling the idea of a flux from last term, we can write the equation for $\tilde{n}$ as
\begin{equation}
    \pd{\tilde{n}}{\tilde{t}} = -\vnabla \cdot \v{J}_n + f(\tilde{n}), \quad \v{J}_n = -D_{\tilde{n}}\vnabla\tilde{n}+ g(\tilde{n},\tilde{c})\vnabla \tilde{c},
\end{equation}
where the first term in $\v{J}_n$ is the usual Fickian flux, and the second can be thought of as a bias along gradients of the chemoattractant $c$. Assuming $g>0$, we see that these two flux terms have opposite signs. This means that the Fickian diffusion term wants to move cells away from regions of high concentration, whereas the chemotactic flux wants to move cells towards regions of high chemoattractant. This description also allows us to see that if we specify Neumann conditions on both $n$ and $c$, we automatically satisfy no-flux for both species, even though the flux in the cell density is not just proportional to gradients of cell density.

While we can perform a linear instability analysis of this general model, we instead consider specific forms of these functions by taking,
\begin{equation}
    f(\tilde{n}) = r \tilde{n}\left (1-\frac{\tilde{n}}{K}\right ), \quad g(\tilde{n},\tilde{c}) = A \tilde{n}, \quad h(\tilde{n},\tilde{c}) = S\tilde{n}.
\end{equation}
Here $A$ is a parameter which alters the strength of the chemotaxis effect, $r$ the cell growth rate, $K$ the carrying capacity of the cells, and $S$ the secretion rate of the chemoattractant by the cells. 

We use the following scalings to nondimensionalise the system
\begin{equation}
\tilde{n} = Kn, \quad \tilde{c} = \frac{SKc}{r}, \quad \tilde{t} = \frac{t}{r}.
\end{equation}
 Substituting the above functional forms of $f$, $g$ and $h$ into \cref{unscaledsnake} and then applying these scalings, we obtain
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\label{scaledsnake}
\pd{{n}}{{t}} &= D_n\nabla^2{n} -\alpha \vnabla\cdot\left(n\vnabla c\right) + n(1-n),\\
\pd{c}{t} &= D_c\nabla^2c + n -  Gc,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $D_n=D_{\tilde{n}}/r$, $D_c=D_{\tilde{c}}/r$, $\alpha = SKA/r^2$, and $G = \gamma/r $ are all positive parameters

The developing embryo of a snake is a coiled cylindrical shape. We will perform the Turing-type linear instability analysis thinking of a general domain, and not worry about the particular form of the eigenfunctions. Rather, we only assume that the boundary conditions are such that the spatial eigenvalues $\rho_k$ which satisfy,
\begin{equation}\label{eigen_snake}
    \nabla^2 w_k + \rho_k w_k = 0,
\end{equation}
are positive-definite. That is, we assume $\rho_0=0$ and $\rho_k>0$ for $k>0$. You might ask: Why are eigenfunctions of the Laplacian important, as the spatial operators in \cref{scaledsnake} are more complicated? As we will see, after linearising the model will always reduce to a linear system involving only Laplacians as the spatial derivatives.

\subsection*{Find the homogeneous equilibria}
The system admits two homogeneous equilibria. We can find these by se 3,tting all time and space derivatives to zero to find,
\begin{equation}
    0 = n_0(1-n_0), \quad 0=n_0 - Gc_0,
\end{equation}
which has the solutions $(n_0, c_0) = (0,0)$ or $(1,1/G)$. As we have seen on Problem Sheet 3, the first of these would lead to non-physical perturbations, and so we only consider perturbations around the second of these.

\subsection*{Linearise the system and solve}
Following the usual procedure, we can substitute $n = n_0 + \varepsilon N$, $c = c_0 + \varepsilon C$ into \cref{scaledsnake} to find a linear system of equations. The first of these involves a nonlinear advection term so we will go through this part in detail. We have,
\begin{equation}\label{chemo_manip_1}
 \pd{}{t}(n_0 + \varepsilon N) = D_n\nabla^2(n_0 + \varepsilon N) -\alpha \vnabla\cdot\left((n_0 + \varepsilon N)\vnabla (c_0 + \varepsilon C)\right) + (n_0 + \varepsilon N)(1-(n_0 + \varepsilon N)).
\end{equation}
Derivatives of $n_0$ and $c_0$ are zero, so we can simplify this equation. We also apply the product rule on the term involving $\alpha$ to find:
\begin{equation}\label{chemo_manip_2}
\varepsilon \pd{N}{t} = \varepsilon D_n\nabla^2 N -\varepsilon \alpha n_0 \nabla^2 C-\varepsilon^2 \alpha \vnabla N \cdot \vnabla C + n_0(1-n_0) + \varepsilon (1-2n_0)N - \varepsilon^2 N^2.
\end{equation}
The terms of order $\varepsilon^2$ will be negligible for a linear analysis, so we will drop these. We also have that $n_0(1-n_0)=0$ at a homogeneous steady state. Dividing by $\varepsilon$ and writing the equation for $C$ (which we note is the same as the equation for $c$, as it was already a linear equation), we have the linearised reaction--diffusion system,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \pd{N}{t} &= D_n\nabla^2 N - \alpha n_0 \nabla^2 C + (1-2n_0)N, \\
    \pd{C}{t} &= D_c\nabla^2C + N -  GC.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As in \cref{turinganalysis}, we can write this in vector notation as,
\begin{equation}\label{linear_snake}
    \pd{}{t}\begin{pmatrix}
    N\\C
    \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
    D_n & -\alpha n_0 \\ 0 & D_c
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\m{D}}\nabla^2\begin{pmatrix}
    N\\C
    \end{pmatrix} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
    1-2n_0 & 0 \\ 1 & -G
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\m{J}}\begin{pmatrix}
    N\\C
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Turing analysis}
We note that \cref{linear_snake} is similar to \cref{linear_turing}, except that here $\m{J}$ does not satisfy the right sign structure of the Jacobian. However, we also see that $\m{D}$ is not diagonal, and it is this feature which will allow the system to admit pattern-forming instabilities. We also note that the steady state $(1,1/G)$ is stable in the absence of diffusion, as the Jacobian evaluated at this state satisfies $\det(\m{J}) = G>0$ and $\tr(\m{J}) = -(1+G)<0$. Henceforth we will take $n_0=1$ and only consider this steady state.

To solve the linear system given by \cref{linear_snake}, we again assume the usual ansatz that $(N,C) \propto \e^{\lambda_k t}w_k(\v{x})$. Substituting this in, we find that the growth rates $\lambda_k$ are eigenvalues of the matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \m{M} = -\rho_k \m{D} + \m{J} = \begin{pmatrix}
    -(1+\rho_k D_n) & \rho_k \alpha  \\ 1 & -(G+\rho_k D_c)
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
In general, for a fixed geometry and hence fixed sequence of $\rho_k$, we must compute the growth rates explicitly to see if any give rise to instability. However, as in \cref{turinganalysis}, we can think of a sufficiently large domain so that $\rho_k$ approximates any continuous value, and then derive conditions on the parameters which are necessary for instability. As in the case of reaction--diffusion systems, we see that $\tr(\m{M}) < \tr(\m{J}) < 0$, and hence stability cannot be lost this way. 

Next let's consider the determinant condition, which we write as,
\begin{equation}
    h(\rho_k) = \det(\m{M}) = (1+\rho_k D_n)(G+\rho_k D_c) - \rho_k \alpha = \rho_k^2 D_c D_n + \rho_k(D_c + G D_n - \alpha) + G.
\end{equation}
Compare this equation to the reaction--diffusion case given in \cref{h_eq}. As in that case, we have that $\alpha > D_c + GD_n$ is a necessary condition for instability, but not a sufficient one. We can find the minimum value of $h$ by taking the derivative with respect to $\rho_k$ to find,
\begin{equation}
    \fd{h}{\rho_k} = 2\rho_k^* D_c D_n + (D_c + G D_n - \alpha) = 0 \implies \rho_k^* = \frac{\alpha -D_c - GD_n}{2D_c D_n}.
\end{equation}
Substituting this into $h$ and rearranging, we find a second condition for instability to be,
\begin{equation}
      h(\rho_k^*)<0 \implies  4G D_c D_n < (\alpha -D_c - GD_n)^2.
\end{equation}
As we have taken especially simple functional forms, these two conditions seem fairly straightforward. In particular, we see that for any fixed positive values of $D_c$, $D_n$, and $G$, we can take $\alpha$ sufficiently large to satisfy these conditions.

We give example simulations of \cref{scaledsnake} in \cref{Snake_Sims}, showing how the domain size can influence the structure of the patterns. In particular we note that, as we have seen for reaction--diffusion simulations, the domain size scales the pattern in a reasonably obvious way (a larger domain supports finer-scale patterns).

\subsection{Geometry, shared modes and alternative snake patterns}
The shape of a long narrow domain also helps interpret the pattern. On a rectangle of length $L_1$ and width $L_2$, no-flux conditions allow
\begin{equation}
\rho_{m,n}=\pi^2\left(m^2/L_1^2+n^2/L_2^2\right).
\end{equation}
Different pairs $(m,n)$ can have the same spatial eigenvalue and therefore the same linear growth rate. For example, if $L_1=20$ and $L_2=1$, the modes $(20,0)$ and $(0,1)$ both have $\rho=\pi^2$, although one varies along the long direction and the other across the short direction. This is a route by which one parameter set can allow differently oriented patterns. Initial perturbations and nonlinear interactions then help determine which is expressed. The linear dispersion relation alone cannot decide between them.

\subsection{General cross-diffusion systems}


Briefly, we note that the above analysis generalises substantially to other kinds of directed transport. We consider the nonlinear cross-diffusion system,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \pd{u}{t} &= \vnabla \cdot \left (d_1(u,v)\vnabla u + d_2(u,v) \vnabla v \right ) + F(u,v), \\
    \pd{v}{t} &= \vnabla \cdot \left (d_3(u,v)\vnabla u + d_4(u,v) \vnabla v \right ) + G(u,v),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the $d_i$ and $F$, $G$ are given sufficiently smooth functions.\footnote{For systems like these, PDE-theory questions of existence and regularity of solutions become quite a bit harder, and blow-up phenomena are much more common. Here we will assume the functions are such that we can neglect these issues, but it is worth noting that one has to choose these nonlinear functions with some care.}  We now that \cref{scaledsnake} is a special case of this model with $d_1=D_u$, $d_3=0$, $d_4=D_v$ and $d_2 = -\alpha u$, and $u$ replaced by $n$, $v$ replace by $c$.  



As in the case of chemotaxis, if we impose Neumann conditions on $u$ and $v$, then this automatically gives no-flux conditions. This can be seen because the flux of $u$, for example, is $\v{J}_u =d_1(u,v)\vnabla u + d_2(u,v) \vnabla v$. The converse, that no-flux implies Neumann conditions, is not in general true. However, it turns out that for all permissible values of $u$ and $v$, we need the matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \m{D} = \begin{pmatrix}
    d_1(u,v) & d_2(u,v) \\ d_3(u,v) & d_4(u,v)
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
to be positive-definite in order to guarantee existence of sufficiently smooth solutions\footnote{This can in principle be relaxed, but we will not discuss this case as it becomes extremely technical.}. One can then show that imposing no-flux conditions must imply Neumann conditions.

Homogeneous equilibria of this system satisfy $F(u_0,v_0)=G(u_0,v_0)=0$. We can perform the usual perturbation analysis for linear stability by setting $u = u_0 + \varepsilon u_1$, $v = v_0 + \varepsilon v_1$. After some careful algebra, we find that the linear perturbations satisfy,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    \pd{u_1}{t} &=  d_1(u_0,v_0)\nabla^2 u_1 + d_2(u_0,v_0) \nabla^2 v_1 + F_u(u_0,v_0)u_1+F_v(u_0,v_0)v_1, \\
    \pd{v_1}{t} &= d_3(u_0,v_0)\nabla^2 u_1 + d_4(u_0,v_0) \nabla^2 v_1 + G_u(u_0,v_0)u_1+G_v(u_0,v_0)v_1.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
As before, we can write this as a system of equations by putting $\v{u}_1 = (u_1,v_1)$. We then have,
\begin{equation}
    \pd{\v{u}_1}{t} = \m{D}\nabla^2\v{u}_1 + \m{J}\v{u}_1, \quad \m{J} = \begin{pmatrix}
    F_u & F_v \\ G_u & G_v 
    \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where the functions in the matrices are evaluated at the homogeneous steady state, $(u_0,v_0)$. For simplicity we will omit this dependence in all of the partial derivatives of $F$ and $G$, and in the $d_i$.

We can expand these perturbations as $\v{u}_1 \propto \e^{\lambda_k t}w_k(\v{x})$, with the $w_k$ again satisfying $\nabla^2 w_k + \rho_k w_k = 0$. Substituting this in, we find that the growth rates $\lambda_k$ are eigenvalues of the matrix,
\begin{equation}
    \m{M} = -\rho_k \m{D} + \m{J} = \begin{pmatrix}
    F_u-\rho_k d_1 & F_v-\rho_k d_2  \\ G_u-\rho_k d_3 & G_v-\rho_k d_4
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
We then have that the growth rates satisfy,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k^2 - \tr(\m{M})\lambda_k + \det(\m{M}) = 0, \quad \tr(\m{M}) = F_u+G_v-\rho_k(d_1+d_4),
\end{equation}
and we have,
\begin{equation}
    h(\rho_k) = \det(\m{M}) = \rho_k^2(d_1d_4-d_2d_3) + \rho_k(d_2G_u+d_3F_v - d_1G_v - d_4F_u) + F_uG_v - F_vG_u.
\end{equation}

We will now derive conditions for pattern formation. We first assume that $\m{J}$ has eigenvalues with negative real part, and hence we have,
\begin{equation}
    \tr(\m{J}) = F_u + G_v < 0,\quad  \det(\m{J}) = F_uG_v - F_vG_u > 0.
\end{equation}
By assumption $\m{D}$ is positive-definite, and hence it must have a positive trace and determinant. So we have,
\begin{equation}
    d_1+d_4 > 0, \quad d_1d_4-d_2d_3 > 0.
\end{equation}
From this last condition we have that the coefficient of $\rho_k^2$ in $h(\rho_k)$ is positive, and the constant term in this function is also equivalent to $\det(\m{J})$, which is positive. We also have that $\tr(\m{M}) < \tr(\m{J}) < 0$, and hence must again focus our attention on values of $\rho_k$ where $h(\rho_k)<0$.

To find the minimum value of $h$ We compute,
\begin{equation}
    \fd{h}{\rho_k} = 2(d_1d_4-d_2d_3)\rho_k^* + d_2G_u+d_3F_v - d_1G_v - d_4F_u = 0,
\end{equation}
and hence,
\begin{equation}
    \rho_k^* = \frac{d_1G_v + d_4F_u-d_2G_u-d_3F_v}{2(d_1d_4-d_2d_3)}.
\end{equation}
For this to be positive we must have,
\begin{equation}
    d_1G_v + d_4F_u > d_2G_u+d_3F_v,
\end{equation}
which is a generalisation of the third Turing condition given in \cref{turingconditions} (in this notation, set $d_1=1$, $d_4=D$, and $d_2=d_3=0$ to exactly recover the result in the reaction--diffusion case).  

Substituting this critical value in and simplifying, we find
\begin{equation}
    h(\rho_k^*) = -\frac{(d_1G_v + d_4F_u-d_2G_u-d_3F_v)^2}{4(d_1d_4-d_2d_3)} + F_uG_v - F_vG_u.
\end{equation}
Hence for $h(\rho_k^*)<0$ we must have,
\begin{equation}
    (d_1G_v + d_4F_u-d_2G_u-d_3F_v)^2-4(d_1d_4-d_2d_3)(F_uG_v - F_vG_u) > 0.
\end{equation}
This is precisely a generalisation of the fourth Turing condition given in \cref{turingconditions}; to see this, again set $d_1=1$, $d_4=D$, and $d_2=d_3=0$ to exactly recover the result in the reaction--diffusion case.

On Problem Sheet 8 there is an example where you will use these conditions to find conditions for pattern formation in a predator--prey model with prey moving towards the predator. Examples of these kinds of \emph{ambush predators} are given in \cref{Ambush_Fig}.

\section{Non-local interactions}
\begin{nem}
The following derivation of the higher-order PDE given by \cref{biharmonic_PDE_pre} will itself be non-examinable. Everything from that equation onward is important for the exam, and more similar to things we have seen before.
\end{nem}

\begin{extra-reading}
\textbf{Relevant reading:} \textsc{Murray}, vol.~I, \S11.5; and vol.~II, chaps~6 and 12 (only a cursory look!)
\end{extra-reading}

So far in this course we have covered models which were either spatially homogeneous (ODEs), or which involved only the Laplacian or relatively simple nonlinear generalisations of it. There are many other model formulations one can use, especially to consider interactions in space. In this section, we will briefly look at models involving integrals of the unknown functions, in addition to partial derivatives. These are sometimes called \emph{integro-differential} equations, and their general theory and behaviour can be quite a lot more exotic than the corresponding case of PDEs or ODEs. Nevertheless, in many useful contexts we can transform these equations into systems of PDEs, and this is the approach we will take in this section. For simplicity we will only consider models of a single species.

Consider the following equation posed on the spatial domain $x \in \mathbb{R}$ for a population $u(x,t)$,
\begin{equation}\label{nonlocal_PDE}
    \pd{u}{t} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(x,y) F(u(y,t)) \, \d y + G(u(x,t)),
\end{equation}
where $F$ and $G$ are suitably nice nonlinear functions, and $K$ is known as a kernel function. You may have seen this terminology used for Green's functions, which are the kernels of certain integral operators. The integral term here represents a general form of interaction between the population at a spatial point $y$, and the population at a point $x$, with the integration being a sum over all of the points which interact with $x$. Generally we choose a kernel $K$ to represent a model for how the population at different spatial points interact, and the function $F$ for the form of the interaction.

We note that such equations can be posed on finite domains, or alternatively the kernel $K$ can have compact support which also makes the integral reduce to one over a bounded domain. Compared to PDEs, such integral equations do not typically require any additional boundary conditions, although we still do require an initial condition due to the derivative in $t$. One can develop a theory of steady states and linear stability analysis for these kinds of equations, though it becomes more technical in general. Instead, we will look at a particular class of kernels which can be reduced to PDEs with higher-order derivatives.

\subsection{Relation to PDEs}

We can relate these nonlocal models to PDEs, like the reaction--diffusion equations we have seen, by choosing a particular form of the kernel $K$ and interaction term $F$ which models a kind of `local' interaction. We set $K(x,y) = K(x-y)=K(y-x)=K(|x-y|)$, so that the kernel is only a function of the distance between two points. We will also assume that this influence decays rapidly as the distance increases; that is $K(x-y) \to 0$ for $|x-y| \gg 1$. An example of such a kernel is,
\begin{equation}\label{example_kernel}
    K(|x-y|) = \e^{-s(x-y)^2}.
\end{equation}
In this case, especially for $s \gg 1$, this function approaches a very sharp and narrow limit around the point $x=y$ (which will increasingly approximate a $\delta$-function for large $s$) so that only nearby regions interact. For the interaction, we simply take the linear function $F(u) = u$.

We will now use our assumptions on this kernel to perform a Taylor series of the integral term in \cref{nonlocal_PDE}. Before we do this, we note some properties of this kind of kernel. By the assumed symmetry of the argument, we see that $K(y)$ is an even function of $y$. Hence we have,
\begin{equation}\label{K_symmetry}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y^{2m+1}K(y)\,\d y = 0, \quad \textrm{for } m=0,1,2,3,\dots
\end{equation}
We also define the \emph{moments} of the kernel (which are just numbers) by,
\begin{equation}
K_{2m} = \frac{1}{(2m)!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}y^{2m}K(y)\, \d y, \quad \textrm{for } m=0,1,2,3,\dots
\end{equation}


Now we are ready to expand the integral term in \cref{nonlocal_PDE}, under the assumption that the kernel decays sufficiently quickly (for example, $s \gg 1$ in \cref{example_kernel}). In this case, we can think of the quantity $z = x-y$ as being `small' and use a Taylor expansion. We have that the integral term looks like,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}\label{horrific_expansion}
    &\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(x-y) u(y,t) \, \d y = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(z) u(x-z,t) \, \d z \\ &=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(z) \left [u(x,t)-z\pd{u}{x}(x,t)+\frac{z^2}{2}\pdd{u}{x}(x,t)-\frac{z^3}{6}\pdn{3}{u}{x}(x,t) + \frac{z^4}{24}\pdn{4}{u}{x}(x,t) +\cdots \right] \d z\\
    &=u(x,t)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} K(z) \, \d z-\pd{u}{x}(x,t)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} zK(z) \, \d z +\pdd{u}{x}(x,t)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{z^2}{2}K(z) \, \d z+\cdots\\
    &=u(x,t)K_0 +\pdd{u}{x}(x,t)K_2+\pdn{4}{u}{x}(x,t)K_4+\pdn{6}{u}{x}(x,t)K_6+\cdots,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where all of the odd terms vanish due to the symmetry of $K$ (see \cref{K_symmetry}). This expansion looks scary, but the point is just that if the integral kernel $K$ is sufficiently smooth and decays quickly enough, then you can approximate the integral term by spatial derivatives.

We note this expansion only makes sense if the $K_{2m}$ get smaller sufficiently quickly. For \cref{example_kernel}, we can compute (not trivial but not immensely hard):
\begin{equation}
    K_{2m} = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{s}(4s)^m m!}.
\end{equation}
These terms tend towards $0$, and generally quite rapidly, though there are some technicalities in ensuring a convergent expansion for \cref{horrific_expansion}.\footnote{Using Stirling's approximation, you can show that the series converges depending on how large the derivative terms become. This is highly technical and not too important for our purposes, but kind of an interesting application of analysis-y methods!} We will not dwell on these, and instead study what happens when we truncate the series. To see how quickly these terms converge we consider $s=1$, and compute that $K_2 \approx 0.443$, $K_4 \approx 0.055$,  $K_6 \approx 0.005$, $K_8 \approx 0.0003$,  etc. We note that the signs of these moments can be different, especially when negative interactions are modelled.

Given the numerical evidence above, and for simplicity, we will truncate this expansion at the fourth order to demonstrate the impact of this kind of nonlocality on instabilities. Our equation then looks like,
\begin{equation}\label{biharmonic_PDE_pre}
    \pd{u}{t} = uK_0 +K_2\pdd{u}{x}+K_4\pdn{4}{u}{x} + G(u).
\end{equation}
We can simplify this by writing $H(u) = uK_0+G(u)$ to find,
\begin{equation}\label{biharmonic_PDE}
    \pd{u}{t} = K_2\pdd{u}{x}+K_4\pdn{4}{u}{x} + H(u).
\end{equation}
We will study this equation assuming that it is posed on a finite spatial domain, $x \in [0,L]$, in order to avoid some technicalities about eigenfunctions of the Laplacian defined on $\mathbb{R}$. This can be made sense of if the above derivation was done on a bounded domain, and the limit taken more carefully near the boundaries. It also helps explain why we think of higher-order derivatives as being less `local,' than lower-order ones.

We can impose any boundary conditions that make biological sense, but as the equation is now higher-order in space, we must have two boundary conditions at each endpoint. We will discuss different choices of these boundary conditions in the next subsection.

You have in fact seen an equation with higher-order spatial derivatives before, namely, the beam equation from Michaelmas. This equation had the form,
\begin{equation}
\pdn{4}{w}{s}+\frac{N}{EI}\pdd{w}{s}+\rho A\pdd{w}{t} = 0,
\end{equation}
(where $w$ was the unknown displacement and $s$ was the spatial variable). This is not quite the same form as \cref{biharmonic_PDE}, as the beam equation has a second order derivative in time, but you can in fact study it using the methods of this section by reducing it to a first-order system.

\subsection{Linearisation and spectral analysis}
We can again study homogeneous equilibria of \cref{biharmonic_PDE} by finding numbers $u_0$ such that $H(u_0)=0$, as these will identically satisfy the right-hand side of the equation being zero. Following the usual procedure of writing $u = u_0 + \varepsilon u_1$ and substituting this into \cref{biharmonic_PDE}, we find that perturbations satisfy,
\begin{equation}
    \pd{u_1}{t} = K_2\pdd{u_1}{x}+K_4\pdn{4}{u_1}{x} + H'(u_0)u_1.
\end{equation}
If we perform a separation-of-variables solution, we can use an ansatz of the form $u_1 = e^{\lambda_k t}f(x)$ to find an eigenvalue equation for the function $f$ of the form,
\begin{equation}\label{eigen_biharmonic}
\lambda_k f = K_2\fdd{f}{x}+K_4\fdn{4}{f}{x} + H'(u_0)f.
\end{equation}
This is a fourth-order ODE which can be solved to find four linearly independent solutions. Using the boundary conditions, as we did in the reaction--diffusion case, we can compute the value of the constant $\lambda_k$. But this quickly becomes very tedious!

Alternatively, the following boundary conditions allow the use of a clever trick:
\begin{equation}\label{biharmonic_BCs}
    \pd{u}{x}(0,t) = 0, \quad \pd{u}{x}(L,t) = 0, \quad \pdn{3}{u}{x}(0,t) = 0, \quad \pdn{3}{u}{x}(L,t) = 0.
\end{equation}
We let $w_k(x) = \cos(k \pi x/L)$ be the usual Laplacian eigenfunctions satisfying Neumann boundary conditions, and recall
\begin{equation}
    \pdd{w_k}{x} + \rho_k w_k = 0,
\end{equation}
with $\rho_k = (k\pi/L)^2$. We then note that these functions $w_k(x)$ satisfy all of the boundary conditions given in \cref{biharmonic_BCs}, and hence we can use the ansatz\footnote{There are other solutions to the eigenfunction equation \cref{eigen_biharmonic} in general, but for these boundary conditions the $w_k$, coming from just the Laplacian, are sufficient to represent any spatial perturbation of the equation.} $u_1 = e^{\lambda_k t}w_k(x)$ to find,
\begin{equation}\label{biharmonic_growth_rates}
\lambda_k  = -\rho_kK_2+\rho_k^2K_4 + H'(u_0).
\end{equation}

If we assume, as in the Turing analysis from preceding chapters, that the system is stable in the absence of transport, then we must have $\lambda_0 = H'(u_0)<0$ at the steady state. We also require that $\Re(\lambda_k)<0$ as $k \to \infty$, as otherwise we obtain instability of arbitrarily fine-scale patterns, which effectively break our assumptions of a spatial continuum. This implies that $K_4<0$. Hence the only possibility for some finite range of $k$ to lead to $\Re(\lambda_k)>0$ is if $K_2<0$.

\subsection{Example: Non-local spatial patterning in a mechanical strain model}
Let's consider an example interaction scheme where we observe spatial pattern formation in a relatively simple system. From the above analysis, we need $K_2<0$ and $K_4<0$. One kind of model which can, for the right parameters, admit these signs is a kernel with oscillations between activation and inhibition in space. That is, we choose a $K$ such that nearby populations benefit one another, but ones farther away inhibit one another. See \cref{fig_kernel} for a sketch of such an interaction kernel. An example is given by the function,
\begin{equation}%\label{example_kernel}
    K(x-y) = \e^{-(x-y)^2}(\cos(x-y)-\alpha).
\end{equation}

This kernel has the moments,
\begin{equation}
    K_2 = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{8}\left(\e^{-\frac{1}{4}}-2\alpha\right), \quad K_4 = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{384}\left(\e^{-\frac{1}{4}}-12\alpha\right),
\end{equation}
which are both negative for  $2\alpha>\e^{-\frac{1}{4}}$.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{images2/Kernel}
    \caption{An example kernel with short-range activation and long-range inhibition structure.}
    \label{fig_kernel}
\end{figure}

An important interpretation of such a structure is that the population $u$ acts as its own activator nearby, but its own inhibitor far away. Hence it can exhibit the same kind of structures we observed in \cref{turinganalysis} for reaction--diffusion systems, but in a single species. Such interactions exist in mechanical and neural interactions, where cells interact over long distances via filopodia or mechanical signals, as well as in flocking and aggregation phenomena. 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.15\textwidth]{images2/MechanicalL5.png}\hspace{1cm}\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{images2/MechanicalL15.png}
    \caption{Simulations of \cref{mechanical} on a square domain with $K_2,K_4<0$ chosen to satisfy the necessary condition for pattern formation. The sides of the domain on the right are three times larger than those on the domain on the left (shown to scale).}
    \label{Mechanical_Sims}
\end{figure}

We consider a simple form of a mechanical interaction as an example of this kind of instability analysis. If we let $u$ denote the deformation of a sheet of cells, such as the epithelium or outermost layer of your skin, we can consider such an interaction kernel with $K_2 = -a <0$ and $K_4 = -b<0$ (with $a,b$ positive) to represent the influence of curvature on the cell layer itself. In the absence of curvature, we assume the cells want to relax to an undeformed state (that is, $u_0=0$ is the only homogeneous equilibrium). A simple model of this would take $H(u) = -u-u^3$. Our equation would then be,
\begin{equation}\label{mechanical}
    \pd{u}{t} = -a\pdd{u}{x}-b\pdn{4}{u}{x} -u-u^3,
\end{equation}
and we see that linearising it is equivalent to just dropping the $-u^3$ term. Hence by \cref{biharmonic_growth_rates}, we have
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k  = \rho_ka-\rho_k^2b -1.
\end{equation}

This dispersion relation is quite simple, and it is easy to calculate, e.g., the range of unstable wavenumbers, and the fastest growing mode. We compute the range of unstable wavenumbers by solving $\lambda_k=0$ as a function of $\rho_k$ to find,
\begin{equation}
\rho_k^\pm = \frac{a\pm \sqrt{a^2-4b}}{2b},
\end{equation}
from which we see that $a^2 > 4b$ is necessary for instability (as otherwise the quadratic given by $\lambda_k$ would never become positive). If this condition is satisfied, then the values of $\rho_k$ for which $\lambda_k=0$ are real and positive.

If we maximise $\lambda_k$ as a function of $\rho_k$ we find the fastest growing mode as,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_k(\rho_k^*) =\lambda_k\left(\frac{a}{2b}\right) = \left(\frac{a^2}{2b}\right)-\left(\frac{a^2}{4b}\right) -1 = \frac{a^2}{4b}-1,
\end{equation}
which is positive as long as $a^2 > 4b$. You can see simulations of this model in \cref{Mechanical_Sims} for two different domain lengths, in order to see how the instability regimes scale with size. Importantly, as with reaction--diffusion systems, the linear analysis can approximately tell you what wavelength the pattern will be, but it cannot say whether spots or stripes form.

\chapter{Transient bacterial patterns: an evolving background}
\label{merged:bacteria}
\begin{extra-reading}
\textbf{Relevant reading:} Murray, vol.~II, chapter 5. Here we extend the method to a time-dependent homogeneous solution.
\end{extra-reading}
Our previous pattern-forming instabilities were calculated around a homogeneous equilibrium. Bacterial aggregation provides a useful reason to go beyond this assumption: an added stimulant changes the chemical environment over time, and observed aggregates can form, become larger, and eventually disappear.

\section{From observations to a model}
This case study distinguishes two experimental regimes. In liquid, aggregation occurs on a timescale short compared with reproduction, so movement is the principal mechanism. In semi-solid conditions, observations over many generations require population growth and nutrient consumption to be included. This distinction should determine the equations before we begin any stability calculation.

Let $n$ denote bacterial density, $c$ a chemoattractant concentration, and $s$ a stimulant or nutrient. A model incorporating diffusion, chemotaxis, growth and consumption is
\begin{align}\label{merged:bacteriafull}
\pd{n}{t}&=D_n\nabla^2n-\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{k_1n}{(k_2+c)^2}\nabla c\right]
+k_3n\left(k_4\frac{s^2}{k_9+s^2}-n\right),\\
\pd{c}{t}&=D_c\nabla^2c+k_5s\frac{n^2}{k_6+n^2}-k_7nc,\\
\pd{s}{t}&=D_s\nabla^2s-k_8n\frac{s^2}{k_9+s^2}.
\end{align}
All coefficients are positive. Each term represents a separate modelling assumption:
\begin{itemize}
\item Bacteria move up the chemoattractant gradient; their sensitivity decreases as $c$ increases through the factor $(k_2+c)^{-2}$.
\item The effective carrying capacity grows with nutrient availability and saturates.
\item Chemoattractant production depends on both the bacterial density and the available stimulant; its dependence on density saturates.
\item Nutrient is depleted by the bacteria, with consumption limited when nutrient is scarce.
\end{itemize}
These functional forms are choices to be checked against observations. A plausible term is not automatically a measured law.

\subsection{Reduction for a short liquid-phase experiment}
To isolate movement-driven aggregation, omit reproduction and death. Suppose also that stimulant consumption and chemoattractant removal are negligible over the observation interval. This gives
\begin{align}\label{liquidphasefull}
\pd{n}{t}&=D_n\nabla^2n-\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{k_1n}{(k_2+c)^2}\nabla c\right],\\
\pd{c}{t}&=D_c\nabla^2c+k_5s\frac{n^2}{k_6+n^2},\\
\pd{s}{t}&=D_s\nabla^2s.
\end{align}
The removal terms have been neglected by a timescale assumption, not by algebra. Uniform initial stimulant $s=s_0$ remains uniform under no-flux conditions, so it can be treated as a constant. With initial mean bacterial density $n_0>0$, use
\begin{equation}
\widehat n=n/n_0,\quad \widehat c=c/k_2,\quad
\widehat t=(k_5s_0/k_2)t,\quad
\widehat x=\sqrt{k_5s_0/(D_ck_2)}\,x,
\end{equation}
and define $D=D_n/D_c$, $\alpha=k_1/(D_ck_2)$ and $\mu=k_6/n_0^2$.
Dropping hats, the one-dimensional system becomes
\begin{align}\label{liquidphase}
\pd{n}{t}&=D\pdd{n}{x}-\alpha\pd{}{x}\left[\frac{n}{(1+c)^2}\pd{c}{x}\right],\\
\pd{c}{t}&=\pdd{c}{x}+\frac{n^2}{\mu+n^2}.
\end{align}
Take $n_x=c_x=0$ at $x=0,L$. These conditions give zero bacterial flux as well as zero chemical flux. Consequently $\int_0^L n\,\mathrm{d}x$ is conserved.

\section{The homogeneous solution is not an equilibrium}
This extends the eigenfunction-expansion method of Mathematical Methods \S4.2. We retain the fixed spatial basis, but linearise about an evolving homogeneous solution. Each mode now satisfies a coupled ODE with time-dependent coefficients; the constant exponential growth rates used for an equilibrium need not apply.
Starting from $n=1$ and $c=0$, there is a spatially uniform solution
\begin{equation}\label{merged:background}
n_b(t)=1,\qquad c_b(t)=\frac{t}{1+\mu}.
\end{equation}
The bacteria remain uniformly distributed while chemoattractant accumulates. Setting all time derivatives to zero would miss this background. Instead, ask whether small spatial disturbances to this \emph{solution} grow during its evolution.

Let $q_m=m\pi/L$. For no-flux boundaries expand
\begin{align}\label{merged:dynamicexpansion}
n(x,t)&=1+\varepsilon\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f_m(t)\cos(q_mx),\\
c(x,t)&=c_b(t)+\varepsilon\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}g_m(t)\cos(q_mx).
\end{align}
We have fixed the mean bacterial density, so its zero-mode perturbation is absent. A spatially uniform offset in $c$ can be treated separately. We do not assume exponential time dependence: the coefficients of the linearised equations will depend on $c_b(t)$.

Because $\partial_xc_b=0$, the linearised chemotactic flux involves only
$(1+c_b)^{-2}\partial_xc_1$. Also,
\begin{equation}
\frac{n^2}{\mu+n^2}=\frac{1}{1+\mu}
+\varepsilon\frac{2\mu}{(1+\mu)^2}n_1+O(\varepsilon^2).
\end{equation}
For each nonzero spatial mode this gives
\begin{align}\label{feq}
\frac{\mathrm{d}f_m}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-Dq_m^2f_m+
\frac{\alpha(1+\mu)^2q_m^2}{(1+\mu+t)^2}g_m,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}g_m}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-q_m^2g_m+
\frac{2\mu}{(1+\mu)^2}f_m.
\end{align}
The spatial eigenfunctions still separate the modes. What has changed is the time evolution within each mode.

\section{A scalar equation for the mode amplitude}
Set $\tau=1+\mu+t$, and for one fixed mode write $q=q_m$, $F=f_m$ and $G=g_m$. Then
\begin{align}\label{modeeq}
F'&=-Dq^2F+\frac{\alpha(1+\mu)^2q^2}{\tau^2}G,\\
G'&=-q^2G+\frac{2\mu}{(1+\mu)^2}F,
\end{align}
where primes denote $\tau$ derivatives. Differentiate the first equation and eliminate $G$ using
\begin{equation}
\frac{\alpha(1+\mu)^2q^2}{\tau^2}G=F'+Dq^2F.
\end{equation}
For $q>0$ the result is
\begin{equation}\label{ftaueq}
F''+\left[q^2(D+1)+\frac{2}{\tau}\right]F'
+q^2\left[Dq^2+\frac{2D}{\tau}-\frac{2\alpha\mu}{\tau^2}\right]F=0.
\end{equation}
This equation is linear but has time-dependent coefficients. There is no single constant growth rate $\lambda(q)$ valid throughout the experiment.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=10cm]{images2/bacteriaPatternsODE.png}
\caption{An example of solutions to \ref{ftaueq} for modes $n=1,5$ with randomise initial conditions. Note the peaks of the mode occur earlier for higher modes.}
\end{figure}

\section{A local approximation and what it can tell us}
Write \cref{ftaueq} as $F''+A(\tau)F'+B(\tau)F=0$, with
\begin{equation}
A=q^2(D+1)+2/\tau,\qquad
B=q^2(Dq^2+2D/\tau-2\alpha\mu/\tau^2).
\end{equation}
One approximation is to freeze $A$ and $B$ near a chosen time. The resulting constant-coefficient equation has local rates
\begin{equation}
\lambda_\pm(\tau)=\tfrac12[-A(\tau)\pm\sqrt{A(\tau)^2-4B(\tau)}].
\end{equation}
Since $A>0$, this frozen scalar equation has a positive rate when $B<0$. This is a diagnostic for the approximation, not a necessary and sufficient theorem about amplification in the original time-dependent system. Its usefulness must be checked against integration of \cref{modeeq} or \cref{ftaueq}.

The positive root of $B(\tau_0)=0$ is
\begin{equation}\label{tau0eq}
\tau_0(q)=\frac{\sqrt{1+2q^2\alpha\mu/D}-1}{q^2}
=\frac{2\alpha\mu/D}{1+\sqrt{1+2q^2\alpha\mu/D}}.
\end{equation}
The second expression shows that $\tau_0$ decreases with $q$. The experiment starts at $\tau_i=1+\mu$, so a nonempty interval with $B<0$ after its start requires
\begin{equation}\label{kmax}
0<q^2<\frac{2}{D(1+\mu)}\left(\frac{\alpha\mu}{1+\mu}-D\right).
\end{equation}
The right-hand side must be positive, and at least one allowed $q_m$ must satisfy the inequality. Thus both chemotactic strength and habitat size affect this diagnostic.

\subsection{Why the crossing time is not the exact peak time}
At a positive strict maximum of $F$, say at $\tau_p$, we have $F'=0$ and $F''<0$, so the exact equation gives $B(\tau_p)>0$. Since $B$ changes from negative to positive at $\tau_0$, such a maximum must occur after $\tau_0$. The crossing can estimate a transition timescale, but must not be identified with the exact peak. The peak also depends on the initial amplitudes.

There is a further useful caution. Freezing the original two-component system gives determinant
$Dq^4-2\alpha\mu q^2/\tau^2$, whereas the frozen scalar equation includes the additional $2Dq^2/\tau$ term. Eliminating a variable involves a time-dependent coefficient, so freezing and elimination do not commute. Either frozen calculation requires validation; solving the time-dependent mode equations avoids this ambiguity.

\subsection{An optional reduced approximation}
If, over a particular interval, $F''$ is negligible compared with the retained terms, then
\begin{equation}\label{approxF}
AF'+BF\simeq0,\qquad
F(\tau)\simeq F(\tau_a)
\exp\left[-\int_{\tau_a}^{\tau}\frac{B(s)}{A(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s\right].
\end{equation}
This integral form is sufficient for interpreting the approximation; it avoids a lengthy partial-fraction calculation. A small value of $B$ at one time does not by itself establish a controlled approximation over the whole trajectory. Compare its shape and amplitude with the full mode equations before using it quantitatively.

\section{Returning to the biological observations}

(a) t=1, a large number of small scale aggregations in density $n$. (b) t=3, the bacterial aggregations begin to merge forming larger scale aggregates. This process continues in (c), t=5 and (d) t=15. Eventually these aggregates disappear.}
\end{figure}

The simulations illustrate the transient patterns. The calculation offers the following interpretation of the observed sequence:
\begin{itemize}
\item Chemotactic feedback can amplify spatial disturbances after stimulant is added.
\item As $c_b$ grows, the sensitivity factor $(1+c_b)^{-2}$ decreases. The aggregation mechanism therefore weakens even though the chemical concentration increases.
\item The diagnostic crossing time is earlier for larger wavenumbers. This is consistent with fine spatial structure fading before coarser structure, although actual mode amplitudes and nonlinear interactions must be checked.
\item For every fixed $q>0$, the matrix in \cref{modeeq} tends to a stable triangular matrix with eigenvalues $-Dq^2$ and $-q^2$. Nonzero modes eventually decay in the linearised model. Conservation fixes the mean bacterial density.
\end{itemize}
This supports a mechanism for transient aggregation, rather than a permanent Turing pattern. It does not prove that every initial perturbation grows, nor that nonlinear merging is fully described by independent linear modes.

The unbounded background $c_b(t)$ is also a reminder of the model's limited time range: chemoattractant removal and resource depletion were neglected. Their eventual importance should be assessed before extrapolating to arbitrarily long times. The value of the model is that it links a specific sequence of observations to explicit assumptions and a mechanism that can be tested.

\chapter{Spiral waves: amplitude, phase and rotating patterns}\label{spiral:chapter}
The previous chapters examined stationary pattern onset and transient aggregation. We now consider a different spatial organisation: a pattern that retains its shape while rotating. This connects the spatial models of this term with the oscillations and limit cycles studied in term one.

\section{Biological waves and the modelling question}
A spiral signalling wave is a moving region of activity; it need not represent organisms moving along a spiral trajectory. In the slime mould example in \cref{spiralmould}, signalling and the cellular response must be distinguished. As with the threshold pictures used earlier in this term, an observed bright region may identify where a signal exceeds a detection threshold.

\begin{figure}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=4cm]{images2/spiralWaves1.png}\quad \includegraphics[width=7cm]{images2/spiralWaves2.png}
\caption{\label{spiralmould}Spiral patterns caused by spiral wave signalling in the slime mould Dictyostelium discoideum.}
\end{center}
\end{figure}

Two kinds of local dynamics should be distinguished. An \emph{oscillatory} medium can sustain repeated local oscillations. An \emph{excitable} medium has a resting state but can undergo a large excursion following a sufficiently strong stimulus, followed by recovery. Both settings can support rotating waves for suitable models and conditions. Here we study an amplitude--phase model associated with oscillatory dynamics; it is not a universal model of all excitable waves.

Unlike the Turing calculation, our immediate task is not to determine which infinitesimal disturbances grow about a homogeneous equilibrium. We seek a particular nonlinear, rotating solution and ask what its equations tell us about its geometry. Existence and stability of that solution are separate questions.

\section{A spatial oscillator model}\label{spiral:fg}
Consider two real fields $u,v$ with equal diffusion coefficient $D>0$:
\begin{align}
\pd{u}{t}&=f(a)u-g(a)v+D\nabla^2u,\\
\pd{v}{t}&=g(a)u+f(a)v+D\nabla^2v,\qquad a=\sqrt{u^2+v^2}.
\end{align}
The fields can represent deviations from a reference state, so they need not themselves be nonnegative concentrations. Introduce $w=u+\mathrm{i}v$ to combine the equations:
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:complex}
\pd{w}{t}=[f(|w|)+\mathrm{i}g(|w|)]w+D\nabla^2w.
\end{equation}
Without spatial variation, writing $w=a\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}$ gives
\[
\dot a=af(a),\qquad \dot\phi=g(a).
\]
Thus $f$ controls amplitude growth and saturation, while $g$ gives the angular frequency in the $(u,v)$ plane. A positive root $a_*$ of $f$ gives a periodic orbit if $g(a_*)\ne0$; it is radially attracting when $a_*f'(a_*)<0$. For example,
\[
f(a)=\mu-a^2,\qquad g(a)=\omega-ba^2
\]
has an attracting amplitude $a_*=\sqrt\mu$ for $\mu>0$, with frequency $\omega-b\mu$ when this is nonzero. Spatial diffusion now couples these local oscillators.

\section{Amplitude and phase equations}\label{spiral:amplitudephase}
Write $w(\v{x},t)=A(\v{x},t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi(\v{x},t)}$, with $A>0$ where the phase is defined. Using the product and chain rules from Mathematical Methods,
\begin{align*}
w_t&=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}(A_t+\mathrm{i}A\phi_t),\\
\nabla^2w&=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\left[\nabla^2A-A|\nabla\phi|^2
+\mathrm{i}\left(2\nabla A\cdot\nabla\phi+A\nabla^2\phi\right)\right].
\end{align*}
Substitution into \cref{spiral:complex} and separation into real and imaginary parts gives
\begin{align}
A_t&=D\nabla^2A+Af(A)-DA|\nabla\phi|^2,\label{spiral:A}\\
\phi_t&=D\nabla^2\phi+g(A)+\frac{2D}{A}\nabla A\cdot\nabla\phi.\label{spiral:phase}
\end{align}
Spatial variation in phase affects the amplitude through the negative term $-DA|\nabla\phi|^2$. Phase evolution also depends on amplitude gradients. This coupling is absent from the local oscillator equations.

The representation becomes singular at $A=0$, where phase has no meaning, even though $u,v$ may be smooth. We will use the original complex field to interpret the centre of a spiral.

\section{A pattern of fixed shape rotating in time}\label{spiral:ansatz}
On a plane, seek
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:form}
A=A(r),\qquad \phi=\Omega t+m\theta+\psi(r),\qquad
m\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.
\end{equation}
The integer condition makes $w$ single-valued when $\theta$ increases by $2\pi$. The sign of $m$ determines the sense of phase winding, and $|m|$ is the number of arms when each phase maximum gives one visible arm. The case $m=0$ describes a different class of radial waves.

For $u=A(r)\cos\phi$, phase maxima satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:arms}
\Omega t+m\theta+\psi(r)=2\pi j,\qquad j\in\mathbb Z.
\end{equation}
At fixed radius, the pattern therefore rotates with angular velocity $-\Omega/m$. The parameter $\Omega$ is the temporal phase frequency, not in general the angular velocity of an arm. Visibility additionally depends on $A(r)$ and any threshold applied to $u$.

For $\psi(r)=qr$ with $q\ne0$, the maxima follow Archimedean spirals: $r$ varies linearly with the unwrapped angle. For $\psi(r)=q\log(r/r_0)$ they follow logarithmic spirals: $r$ varies exponentially with angle. These choices illustrate geometry; they are not automatically solutions of the reaction--diffusion equations.

\subsection{Seeing the choices of amplitude and phase}
The earlier notes contained a useful set of pictures which we retain here because they make the role of the radial phase immediately visible.  In all three panels we take $A(r)=r$ and alter only $\psi(r)$.  At a fixed time the phase-maxima curves are determined by
\[
 m\theta+\psi(r)=2\pi j-\Omega t,
\]
so changing $\psi$ changes the geometry of the arms without changing their number.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=0.31\textwidth]{images2/spiralWaves1.pdf}}\quad
\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=0.31\textwidth]{images2/spiralWaves2.pdf}}\quad
\subfloat[]{\includegraphics[width=0.31\textwidth]{images2/spiralWaves3.pdf}}
\caption{Spiral patterns for $u$ obtained using $A(r)=r$ and $\psi(r)=r$ (a), $\psi(r)=\ln r$ (b) and $\psi(r)=\sin(r)$ (c).  These are geometric examples of the ansatz rather than solutions of the reaction--diffusion equations.}
\label{spiral:radialexamples}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
\item With $\psi(r)=r$, equal phase increments correspond to equal radial increments. Along a fixed ray successive maxima have radial separation $2\pi$. The arms form Archimedean spirals.
\item With $\psi(r)=\ln r$, solving the phase condition gives $r=\exp(2\pi j-\Omega t-m\theta)$. Successive maxima along a ray are separated by a fixed \emph{ratio} of radii, rather than a fixed distance. These are logarithmic spirals, not Archimedean spirals. This is a geometric illustration for $r>0$; it is not a claim of a regular solution at the origin.
\item With $\psi(r)=\sin r$, the radial phase is bounded and reverses direction as $r$ increases. The resulting curves bend back and forth rather than winding with a constant radial spacing. This shows how strongly the phase choice constrains pattern shape.
\end{itemize}

Amplitude has a different role. Changing $A(r)$ while it remains positive does not move the phase-maxima curves, but changes the signal strength along them. With $A(r)=r$, the signal grows indefinitely outward; with $A(r)=r/\sqrt{1+r^2}$ it grows from zero and saturates. A detection threshold $u>u_c>0$ makes this distinction visible: at radii where $A(r)\leq u_c$ no arm is visible, while elsewhere the visible region satisfies
\[
\cos(\Omega t+m\theta+\psi(r))>\frac{u_c}{A(r)}.
\]
Thus amplitude affects the extent and width of a thresholded arm, whereas $\psi$ controls its phase geometry. The choices of $A$ and $\psi$ in a true solution must also satisfy the radial equations below.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images2/spiral_rotation_examples.pdf}
\caption{Three snapshots of the geometric field $u=[r/\sqrt{1+r^2}]\cos(t+\theta+r)$, with $m=1$ and $\Omega=1$. Red and blue indicate positive and negative values. The shape rotates clockwise with angular velocity $-1$ while its radial amplitude profile stays fixed. These are plots of the ansatz, not numerical solutions of a reaction--diffusion system.}
\label{spiral:rotationexamples}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{spiral:rotationexamples} isolates the time dependence. At fixed $r$, an individual arm satisfies $\theta(t)=\theta(0)-\Omega t/m$. Reversing $\Omega$ reverses rotation; changing $|m|$ changes the number of arms and, for fixed $\Omega$, the angular speed. More generally, replacing $\Omega t$ by a prescribed function $\chi(t)$ would give instantaneous angular velocity $-\chi'(t)/m$. Constant $\Omega$ is the particular choice that describes rigid rotation at constant speed.

\subsection{Equations for the radial profile}
Recall the polar differential operators used in Mathematical Methods \S2.4.1. For the ansatz in \cref{spiral:form},
\[
\nabla^2A=A''+\frac{A'}r,\qquad
|\nabla\phi|^2=(\psi')^2+\frac{m^2}{r^2},\qquad
\nabla^2\phi=\psi''+\frac{\psi'}r.
\]
Consequently
\begin{align}
0&=D\left(A''+\frac{A'}r-\frac{m^2A}{r^2}\right)
+Af(A)-DA(\psi')^2,\label{spiral:radialA}\\
\Omega&=g(A)+D\left[\psi''+\left(\frac1r+\frac{2A'}A\right)\psi'\right].\label{spiral:radialphase}
\end{align}
We have reduced the rotating PDE problem to coupled nonlinear radial ODEs. This is a new use of the familiar polar operators: we are studying a nonlinear coherent pattern, rather than computing Laplacian eigenfunctions for linear stability.

Multiplying \cref{spiral:radialphase} by $rA^2$ gives the useful divergence form
\[
D(rA^2\psi')'=rA^2[\Omega-g(A)].
\]
For a regular core with $rA^2\psi'\to0$ as $r\to0$, integration yields
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:integral}
\psi'(r)=\frac{1}{DrA(r)^2}\int_0^r sA(s)^2[\Omega-g(A(s))]\,\mathrm{d}s.
\end{equation}
This relates the radial change of phase to the amplitude profile; it does not by itself solve for that profile.

\subsection{The core and the far field}
For $m\ne0$, continuity of $w$ at the origin requires $A(0)=0$: otherwise approaching the same point from different angles gives different values of $w$. If $f,g$ are regular near zero and $\psi'$ remains bounded, substitute $A\sim Cr^s$ into the most singular terms of \cref{spiral:radialA}. Then
\[
s(s-1)+s-m^2=0,\qquad s=\pm|m|.
\]
The regular branch is
\begin{equation}
A(r)\sim Cr^{|m|}\quad (r\to0).
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{spiral:integral} then gives, under these assumptions,
\[
\psi'(r)\sim\frac{\Omega-g(0)}{2D(|m|+1)}r.
\]
Thus the amplitude vanishes at the centre while the phase variation remains compatible with a smooth core.

On an unbounded domain, suppose $A\to A_\infty>0$, $\psi'\to q_\infty$, and the derivative terms in \cref{spiral:radialA,spiral:radialphase} vanish in the limit. The limiting equations require
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:farfield}
\Omega=g(A_\infty),\qquad Dq_\infty^2=f(A_\infty).
\end{equation}
When $q_\infty\ne0$, successive phase maxima along a fixed radial line are asymptotically separated by $2\pi/|q_\infty|$. These are necessary consistency conditions; matching the core to the far field is still needed to select a solution. On a finite disc, outer boundary conditions replace the far-field assumptions. For example, no-flux conditions give $A'=0$ and $\psi'=0$ at the outer edge wherever $A>0$.

\begin{do-it-yourself}
For $f(A)=\mu-A^2$ and $g(A)=\omega-bA^2$, use \cref{spiral:farfield} to relate $A_\infty$, $q_\infty$ and $\Omega$. Explain why these algebraic relations do not prove that a regular rotating spiral exists, or that it is stable.
\end{do-it-yourself}

\section{What this calculation explains}
The amplitude--phase formulation connects local oscillations to spatial organisation. The phase winding determines arm number, the temporal phase determines rotation, and radial phase variation determines spacing. The nonlinear equations constrain those quantities through the amplitude profile. A complete biological interpretation must also specify how the model fields relate to measurable signals and cellular responses.

Unlike the Turing calculation, this construction has not established growth from a nearly uniform state. Nor has it established stability of the rotating solution. It supplies a structured family of possible solutions whose existence, selection and stability can be investigated analytically or numerically.

\section{Why does the $f$--$g$ model describe much more general systems? Non-examinable}\label{spiral:shortfredholm}
\begin{nem}
\textbf{This entire section is non-examinable.  Its purpose is interpretation, not additional examinable technique.}

\medskip
\textbf{What sort of systems are we thinking about?}  Many spatial models contain two or more interacting quantities which react locally and diffuse through space.  Examples include chemical oscillators such as the Belousov--Zhabotinsky reaction, biochemical signalling systems, electrical activity in cardiac tissue, and models of activation and recovery such as FitzHugh--Nagumo-type systems.  The detailed equations in these applications can look very different.  What matters here is that, for some parameter value, the \emph{local} dynamics are close to the onset of a sustained oscillation: a Hopf bifurcation.  Spiral waves can also occur in excitable systems far from a Hopf bifurcation, so the argument below is not a theory of every possible spiral wave.

\medskip
\textbf{What does ``weakly nonlinear'' mean?}  Linearisation tells us when a steady state first becomes oscillatory and gives the leading frequency and shape of that oscillation.  But once the oscillation begins to grow, linear theory cannot tell us what stops the growth.  A weakly nonlinear calculation assumes that we are only a small distance beyond the instability threshold, so the oscillation has small amplitude.  We introduce a small parameter $\varepsilon$ measuring that distance and retain just enough nonlinear terms to describe the first departure from linear behaviour.  These are the terms which normally determine whether the growing oscillation saturates at a finite amplitude and how its amplitude and phase vary slowly in space and time.

\medskip
\textbf{Why do this?}  The reward is that, close to oscillatory onset, many different reaction--diffusion systems have the same leading amplitude equation.  The chemistry, electrophysiology or biology changes the numerical coefficients, but not the basic mathematical structure.  In the important real-diffusion case this reduced equation has exactly the amplitude--phase form of the $f$--$g$ model studied above.  So the apparently special spiral calculation is actually a local model for a much wider class of systems near a Hopf bifurcation.

\medskip
The Fredholm alternative from Mathematical Methods enters only as the mechanism which makes this reduction possible: at the next order of approximation the correction equation can be solved only if its resonant forcing is orthogonal to the adjoint oscillatory mode.  That compatibility condition becomes the evolution equation for the slowly varying amplitude.  The detailed calculation is given in the non-examinable appendix, \cref{spiral:appendix}.
\end{nem}

\subsection*{A broad class of reaction--diffusion systems}
Consider, for example,
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:generalclass}
\begin{aligned}
u_t&=D_u\nabla^2u+F(u,v;\mu),\\
v_t&=D_v\nabla^2v+G(u,v;\mu),\qquad D_u,D_v>0.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The functions $F$ and $G$ may represent quite different mechanisms in different applications.  A FitzHugh--Nagumo-type model, for instance, couples a rapidly responding activation variable to a slower recovery variable.  Related two-variable reaction--diffusion systems are used in chemical oscillations and in models of propagating electrical or biochemical activity.  Nothing here requires the exact rotational symmetry built into the $f$--$g$ equations.

The important assumption is instead about the \emph{regime}.  Suppose there is a homogeneous equilibrium $(u_*(\mu),v_*(\mu))$ and, at $\mu=0$, the kinetic Jacobian has eigenvalues
\[
\lambda=\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_0,\qquad \omega_0>0,
\]
which cross the imaginary axis as $\mu$ varies.  This is the local signature of a Hopf bifurcation.  For two variables,
\[
\operatorname{tr}J_0=0,\qquad \det J_0=\omega_0^2>0.
\]
Near this point the local motion is approximately sinusoidal with frequency $\omega_0$, but the linearised equations alone do not determine the slowly changing size or phase of the oscillation.

\subsection*{The idea of the weakly nonlinear expansion}
Measure the distance from onset with a small number $\varepsilon$.  A standard scaling is
\[
\mu=\varepsilon^2\nu,\qquad \mathbf X=\varepsilon\v{x},\qquad T=\varepsilon^2t.
\]
The original time $t$ describes the rapid oscillation, while $T$ and $\mathbf X$ describe slow changes of its envelope.  If
$J_0\mathbf q=\mathrm{i}\omega_0\mathbf q$, the leading disturbance has the form
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:shortleading}
\mathbf U=
\varepsilon\left[Z(\mathbf X,T)\mathbf q\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t}
+\bar Z\bar{\mathbf q}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_0t}\right]
+O(\varepsilon^2).
\end{equation}
Here $Z$ is a complex \emph{slow amplitude}.  Its modulus tells us the slowly varying strength of the oscillation and its argument gives a slowly varying phase correction.  Thus the calculation separates two effects which occur on very different scales:
\begin{itemize}
\item rapid local oscillation at frequency approximately $\omega_0$;
\item slow modulation of its amplitude and phase across the tissue, chemical medium or other spatial system.
\end{itemize}
That separation is the central idea of the weakly nonlinear analysis.

\subsection*{Why a solvability condition appears}
At leading order the oscillatory mode lies in the kernel of the linear operator.  At higher orders the nonlinear terms, diffusion and the small change in parameter all act as forcing.  Most of that forcing produces an ordinary bounded correction.  The dangerous part is forcing at exactly the natural frequency $\omega_0$: just as a forced oscillator resonates when driven at its own frequency, this contribution would generate a term which grows with the fast time and destroy the assumed expansion.

At order $\varepsilon^3$ the correction has schematic form
\[
\mathcal L\mathbf U_3=\mathbf H,
\qquad \mathcal L=\partial_t-J_0.
\]
If $\mathbf p$ is the adjoint oscillatory eigenvector, the Fredholm alternative says that the resonant part is solvable only when
\[
\mathbf p^\dagger\mathbf H_1=0.
\]
This is the same orthogonality-to-the-adjoint-kernel idea encountered in Mathematical Methods.  Here it has an important new interpretation: we do not merely check the condition after $Z$ has been chosen.  Instead, the condition \emph{determines how $Z$ must evolve} so that the perturbation expansion remains valid.

The result is a complex amplitude equation of the form
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:shortcgle}
Z_T=\alpha\nu Z+d\nabla_{\mathbf X}^2Z+c|Z|^2Z,
\end{equation}
where the coefficients $\alpha,d,c$ are calculated from the original reaction laws and diffusion coefficients.  The first term describes growth or decay as the bifurcation is crossed, the diffusion term couples neighbouring oscillators, and the cubic term is the leading nonlinear correction which can saturate the growth.

\subsection*{Connection with the spiral model}
If $d>0$ is real, write $Z=R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}$.  Then \cref{spiral:shortcgle} becomes
\[
R_T=d\nabla^2R-dR|\nabla\Theta|^2+R f(R),
\]
\[
\Theta_T=d\nabla^2\Theta+rac{2d}{R}\nabla R\cdot\nabla\Theta+g(R),
\]
for suitable functions $f$ and $g$ determined by $\alpha$ and $c$.  These are precisely the amplitude--phase equations developed in \cref{spiral:A,spiral:phase}.  A rotating-wave ansatz for $Z$ therefore leads back to the same radial spiral equations.

This is the main conclusion.  The $f$--$g$ model is useful not because every biological or chemical system literally has that form, but because \emph{near a generic oscillatory onset} it captures the leading slow dynamics of a much wider family of nonlinear reaction--diffusion systems.  The approximation is local in parameter space and describes small, slowly modulated oscillations.  It does not prove that a spiral exists or is stable, and it does not cover all excitable-wave mechanisms, but it explains why studying this comparatively simple system has relevance far beyond the special equations with which we began.

\section*{Perspective}
This term has connected familiar eigenfunction methods to population survival, Turing instability, geometry-dependent pattern selection, directed transport and transient aggregation. Rotating waves extend that narrative to nonlinear patterns with persistent motion. In each case we distinguish the model assumptions, the conclusions established by the mathematics, and the further interpretation needed to compare with observations.

\chapter{Weakly nonlinear oscillations and Fredholm solvability: non-examinable}\label{spiral:appendix}
\begin{info}
\textbf{This entire appendix is non-examinable.} It expands \cref{spiral:shortfredholm}; none of its perturbation calculations or coefficient formulae is required for assessment. The connection to Mathematical Methods \S3.4 is the solvability condition for a linear problem with a nontrivial kernel.
\end{info}

\section{Assumptions and a consistent scaling}
Consider a smooth real two-component reaction--diffusion system. Subtract its smooth homogeneous equilibrium branch and write the deviation as $\mathbf U$, so that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf U_t=\mathcal D\nabla^2\mathbf U+J(\mu)\mathbf U
+\frac12B(\mathbf U,\mathbf U)+\frac16C(\mathbf U,\mathbf U,\mathbf U)+\cdots.
\end{equation}
Here $\mathcal D$ is a constant real diffusion matrix. The symmetric multilinear maps $B,C$ are the second and third derivatives of the reaction vector at the critical equilibrium and parameter; extend them complex-linearly when using complex vectors. We assume enough smoothness for this Taylor expansion. Parameter dependence of $B,C$ contributes only at higher orders in the scaling below.

At $\mu=0$, assume $J_0=J(0)$ has a simple pair $\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_0$, with $\omega_0>0$. Choose right and adjoint eigenvectors satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:adjoint}
J_0\mathbf q=\mathrm{i}\omega_0\mathbf q,\qquad
J_0^\dagger\mathbf p=-\mathrm{i}\omega_0\mathbf p,\qquad
\mathbf p^\dagger\mathbf q=1.
\end{equation}
The dagger denotes conjugate transpose. The adjoint vector projects forcing onto the resonant mode. In higher-dimensional kinetic systems one also requires that other kinetic modes are separated from the critical pair; we keep the two-component case here.

Set
\[
\mu=\varepsilon^2\nu,\qquad \mathbf X=\varepsilon\v{x},\qquad
T=\varepsilon^2t,\qquad
\mathbf U=\varepsilon\mathbf U_1+\varepsilon^2\mathbf U_2+\varepsilon^3\mathbf U_3+\cdots.
\]
Write $J(\mu)=J_0+\mu J_1+O(\mu^2)$. The cubic saturation, parameter-dependent linear growth and slow diffusion all first enter at order $\varepsilon^3$. We seek long spatial modulations on the plane or on a compatible large domain; on a fixed small domain the available modes may not support this spatial scaling.

Let $t_0=t$ denote the fast time. Then $\partial_t=\partial_{t_0}+\varepsilon^2\partial_T$ and $\nabla_{\v{x}}^2=\varepsilon^2\nabla_{\mathbf X}^2$. At each order we seek a correction periodic in $t_0$ with period $2\pi/\omega_0$, and define
\[
\mathcal L=\partial_{t_0}-J_0.
\]

\section{The first two orders}
At order $\varepsilon$, $\mathcal L\mathbf U_1=0$, hence
\begin{equation}
\mathbf U_1=Z(\mathbf X,T)\mathbf q\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}
+\bar Z\,\bar{\mathbf q}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}.
\end{equation}
At order $\varepsilon^2$,
\[
\mathcal L\mathbf U_2=\frac12B(\mathbf U_1,\mathbf U_1).
\]
The forcing contains a mean and second harmonics, so choose
\[
\mathbf U_2=|Z|^2\mathbf h_{11}
+Z^2\mathbf h_{20}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}
+\bar Z^2\bar{\mathbf h}_{20}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0},
\]
where
\begin{align}
-J_0\mathbf h_{11}&=B(\mathbf q,\bar{\mathbf q}),\\
(2\mathrm{i}\omega_0I-J_0)\mathbf h_{20}&=\tfrac12B(\mathbf q,\mathbf q).
\end{align}
These matrices are invertible in the two-component setting assumed here. A homogeneous first-harmonic contribution can be absorbed into the definition of $Z$. The second-order correction matters: quadratic reaction terms can affect the final cubic amplitude coefficient through this correction.

\section{Third order: where Fredholm enters}
At order $\varepsilon^3$,
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:orderthree}
\mathcal L\mathbf U_3=
-\partial_T\mathbf U_1+\nu J_1\mathbf U_1
+\mathcal D\nabla_{\mathbf X}^2\mathbf U_1
+B(\mathbf U_1,\mathbf U_2)+\tfrac16C(\mathbf U_1,\mathbf U_1,\mathbf U_1).
\end{equation}
For periodic vector functions use the inner product
\[
\langle\mathbf a,\mathbf b\rangle=
\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi/\omega_0}\mathbf a(t_0)^\dagger\mathbf b(t_0)\,\mathrm{d}t_0.
\]
Integration by parts has no boundary term because the functions are periodic. Therefore
\[
\mathcal L^\dagger=-\partial_{t_0}-J_0^\dagger,\qquad
\mathcal L^\dagger(\mathbf p\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0})=0.
\]
The Fredholm condition from Mathematical Methods \S3.4 says that the right-hand side of \cref{spiral:orderthree} must be orthogonal to this adjoint nullvector and its conjugate. Equivalently, if $\mathbf H_1$ is its coefficient of $\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}$, then
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:fredholm}
\mathbf p^\dagger\mathbf H_1=0.
\end{equation}
One can also see this as the finite-dimensional solvability condition for
$(\mathrm{i}\omega_0I-J_0)\mathbf z=\mathbf H_1$: left multiplication by $\mathbf p^\dagger$ gives zero on the left. If the condition fails, a resonant forcing creates a secular term proportional to $t_0\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}$ instead of the bounded periodic correction sought here.

Collect the first temporal harmonic in \cref{spiral:orderthree}. Its nonlinear part is
\[
|Z|^2Z\left[B(\mathbf q,\mathbf h_{11})+B(\bar{\mathbf q},\mathbf h_{20})
+\tfrac12C(\mathbf q,\mathbf q,\bar{\mathbf q})\right].
\]
Using $\mathbf p^\dagger\mathbf q=1$, the solvability condition becomes
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:cgle}
Z_T=\alpha\nu Z+d\nabla_{\mathbf X}^2Z+c|Z|^2Z,
\end{equation}
with
\begin{align}
\alpha&=\mathbf p^\dagger J_1\mathbf q,\qquad
 d=\mathbf p^\dagger\mathcal D\mathbf q,\\
c&=\mathbf p^\dagger\left[B(\mathbf q,\mathbf h_{11})
+B(\bar{\mathbf q},\mathbf h_{20})+\tfrac12C(\mathbf q,\mathbf q,\bar{\mathbf q})\right].
\end{align}
This is a leading complex amplitude equation, commonly called a complex Ginzburg--Landau equation. Its coefficients are generally complex. A nondegenerate Hopf crossing requires $\Re\alpha\ne0$, and a nondegenerate cubic saturation requires $\Re c\ne0$. Their signs determine on which side the small homogeneous oscillation branch occurs and whether its radial dynamics are attracting at this order.

\section{A worked projection without quadratic algebra}
To see the solvability calculation explicitly, take
\begin{align}
u_t&=\mu u-\omega_0v-a u^3+D_1\nabla^2u,\\
v_t&=\omega_0u+\mu v-b u^3+D_2\nabla^2v,
\end{align}
where $a>0$, $\omega_0>0$, $D_1,D_2>0$ and $b$ is real. This is an illustrative local oscillator model, not a fitted biological reaction scheme. Its nonlinear terms do not have the rotationally symmetric $f$--$g$ form.

Here
\[
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_0\\\omega_0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf q=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf p=\tfrac12\mathbf q,\qquad J_1=I.
\]
There are no quadratic terms, so we may take $\mathbf U_2=0$. The leading first component is
$u_1=Z\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}+\bar Z\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega_0t_0}$.
Its cube has first-harmonic coefficient $3|Z|^2Z$. Hence the nonlinear resonant forcing is
\[
-3\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}|Z|^2Z,
\]
and its projection is
\[
\tfrac12(1,\mathrm{i})\left[-3\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}\right]|Z|^2Z
=-\tfrac32(a+\mathrm{i}b)|Z|^2Z.
\]
Likewise $\alpha=1$ and $d=(D_1+D_2)/2$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{spiral:worked}
Z_T=\nu Z+\frac{D_1+D_2}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf X}^2Z
-\frac32(a+\mathrm{i}b)|Z|^2Z.
\end{equation}
Writing $Z=R\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\Theta}$ gives exactly the amplitude--phase structure of \cref{spiral:A,spiral:phase}, on the slow scales, with
\[
f(R)=\nu-\tfrac32aR^2,\qquad g(R)=-\tfrac32bR^2,
\qquad D=\tfrac12(D_1+D_2).
\]
For $\nu>0$, the spatially homogeneous amplitude has an attracting positive value $R^2=2\nu/(3a)$. With this eigenvector normalisation, the physical leading fields are
\[
u\approx2\varepsilon R\cos(\omega_0t+\Theta),\qquad
v\approx2\varepsilon R\sin(\omega_0t+\Theta).
\]
The slow phase accounts for a small correction to the fast oscillation frequency. Establishing a rotating spiral still requires solving the spatial amplitude equation with appropriate conditions; the homogeneous amplitude result alone does not do this.

\section{What is shared, and what remains model-dependent}
The example shows how a system whose nonlinear reactions lack the exact $f$--$g$ symmetry nevertheless produces that structure at leading order near oscillatory onset. The general projection explains how quadratic terms and other reaction laws alter the coefficients rather than requiring an entirely new type of leading equation.

The equal-diffusion case $\mathcal D=DI$ always gives $d=D$ with our normalisation. For a general diffusion matrix and general kinetics, $d$ may be complex, so the real-diffusion model in the main chapter is a restricted case. The reduced equation describes small, slowly modulated oscillations near the assumed bifurcation; it is not an exact rewriting of the original PDE. This formal expansion alone provides neither a global error bound nor a proof of spiral existence or stability.

The lesson from Fredholm is precise: projecting out the resonant forcing determines how the amplitude must evolve for the next correction to be solvable. That connection between a linear solvability theorem and a nonlinear evolution equation is the purpose of this appendix.
